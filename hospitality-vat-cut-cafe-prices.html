Hospitality VAT cut to 10%? What the 28 October Budget could mean for your café

The campaign for a 10% rate is louder than it has ever been, and the Prime Minister has said warm things about it. That is not the same as it happening. Here is what is actually confirmed, what a cut would be worth per cup, whether it would reach you at all, and the one decision worth making before Budget day.

Last updated 28 September 2026 · UK-wide (VAT is not devolved) · Not tax advice — check your own position with your accountant

£

Will hospitality VAT be cut to 10% in the 2026 Budget, and what would it mean for my café? As of 28 September 2026 it is a campaign, not a policy — nothing has been announced, and the Budget on 28 October is the first real date for a decision. If it did happen and you held your prices, it would be worth 7.576% of your VAT-inclusive takings on the affected sales: about 27p on a £3.60 latte. But if your takings are under £90,000 you are not VAT-registered and it is worth nothing to you.

Before Budget day

What is actually confirmed, and what is not

The gap between the headlines and the announcements is wide this year. It is worth being precise about which is which, because one of them is something you can plan around and the other is not.

The Budget date
Wed 28 Oct

Confirmed. Chancellor John Healey delivers it, and has said it will be “built on fiscal discipline”. That phrasing is not usually the overture to a £10bn tax cut, but the date is the date, and it is when you will know.

The 10% VAT campaign
Not policy

More than 800 hospitality businesses have signed an open letter to the Prime Minister asking for 20% to come down to 10%, and the #VATsTheProblem petition has passed 370,000 signatures. Andy Burnham said in February, as mayor of Greater Manchester, that he would argue for a rate “more consistent with what you find in Europe”. None of that is a commitment from the Treasury.

Why Europe keeps coming up
10% vs 20%

France, Italy and Spain charge hospitality 10%. Germany charges 7%. The UK charges the full 20%, which is the whole basis of the campaign’s argument and the reason it will not go away whatever happens in October.

What was confirmed — and excludes you
Not cafés

On 23 July the government confirmed a 20% cut to business rates bills for pubs, social clubs and live music venues in England from April 2027. Around 32,000 venues benefit, at roughly £1,100 a year for a typical pub. Grant Thornton’s summary is blunt: “Hotels, restaurants, cafes and cinemas are excluded.” If you run a café-bar, the government has not yet said how hybrid premises will be treated.

One other thing worth not misreading: the VAT cut on electricity from 1 October 2026 is on household bills, taking 5% to 0% for six months. It is not a cut on business energy, and it will not show up on your supply.

Ask this first

Would a cut reach you at all?

This is the question almost every article on the subject skips, and for a lot of small cafés the answer is no.

If you are not VAT-registered, a VAT cut is worth exactly nothing

You must register for VAT once your taxable turnover passes £90,000 in any rolling twelve months. Below that, registration is optional and most small cafés do not bother, because registering means charging VAT on sales that are currently VAT-free to the customer. If you are not registered, you are not charging the 20%, so dropping it to 10% changes nothing at your till. Tax Policy Associates puts the share of hospitality businesses that would see no benefit at all for this reason at 45%.

A rough sense of where the line sits

Selling at an average £3.60 a head, £90,000 is about 25,000 sales a year — very roughly 70 a day if you open seven days. Below that sort of volume you are probably under the threshold and this whole debate is happening to other people. Be careful with the arithmetic, though: taxable turnover counts your total takings, including zero-rated sales like cold takeaway food, not just the coffee. Your accountant can tell you in a minute where you actually stand.

And if you are registered, it is worth less than the headlines suggest

Tax Policy Associates estimates the total cost of a 10% hospitality rate at around £10.5bn a year, of which roughly £4.85bn goes to large chains — McDonald’s alone over £400m. The signatories of that open letter include Wetherspoon, Greene King, Fuller’s, PizzaExpress and Wagamama. They are not wrong to want it. It is just worth knowing that the loudest voices in the campaign are the ones who would collect the largest share of it, and that your slice is your own turnover and nothing more.

Worked through

What a 10% rate would mean per cup

A £3.60 latte today is £3.00 for you and 60p for HMRC. Below is what changes at 10%, depending on what you do with the price. These are illustrative figures on one illustrative cup — put your own price in and the shape holds.

What you do Shelf price VAT You keep Change for you Change for the customer
Today, at 20% £3.60 £0.60 £3.00 — —
Hold the price £3.60 £0.33 £3.27 +27.3p Nothing
Pass it on in full £3.30 £0.30 £3.00 Nothing −30p
Split it £3.45 £0.31 £3.14 +13.6p −15p

VAT-inclusive prices. At 20% the VAT is one sixth of the shelf price; at 10% it is one eleventh. Holding the price is worth 7.576% of the VAT-inclusive price — the same percentage whatever you charge, and the figure Tax Policy Associates uses.

And over a week and a year, if you hold your prices

Same £3.60 cup, open seven days, on coffee alone. Illustrative.

Cups a day Extra per day Extra per week Extra per year
150 £41 £286 ≈ £14,900
300 £82 £573 ≈ £29,800

27.3p a cup × cups × 7 days × 52 weeks, rounded. Coffee only — add your food and the number grows, subject to the eat-in rules below. On operating margins of 3–15%, which is where UK independent cafés have sat for several years, a five-figure sum landing straight on the bottom line is not a rounding error. It is also why the decision below is worth thinking about now rather than on 29 October.

The bit most people get wrong

Eat in or take away: it changes nothing for coffee, and everything for food

If you have ever been told the takeaway rule is how you save VAT on drinks, it is not true, and it is worth knowing why before you plan around a rate change.

Hot drinks
Always 20%

HMRC’s wording is short: “Hot takeaway drinks are standard-rated.” Drink it in and it is standard-rated as catering. Take it out and it is standard-rated as a hot drink. There is no version of a latte that is not 20%, so the whole of your coffee revenue is exposed to the rate — which is exactly why a cut would be worth real money to you.

Cold food, taken away
Usually 0%

Cold takeaway food is normally zero-rated already, with the familiar exceptions — crisps, sweets, bottled water and some other drinks are standard-rated whatever you do. A cut to 10% would not touch the zero-rated part of your menu, because there is nothing there to cut.

Anything eaten in
20%

The moment food is consumed on your premises it is catering and standard-rated, cold or not. The same sandwich is zero-rated in a bag and standard-rated on a plate at your table.

What that means for you
Know your mix

Two cafés with identical takings can gain very different amounts from the same VAT cut. A sit-in café serving hot drinks and plated food has nearly all of its revenue at 20%. A takeaway-led kiosk selling coffee and cold pastries in bags has a chunk that is already zero-rated. Pull the split between standard-rated and zero-rated sales off your till before Budget day — that ratio is what decides what a cut is worth to you, and you will not be able to work it out on the day.

The actual decision

Should you pass it on? Three options, and who each one suits

Nobody can make you pass a VAT cut on, and history says most businesses do not pass on all of it. When Ireland cut hospitality VAT from 13.5% to 9% in 2011, research cited by Tax Policy Associates found only about half of it reached consumers as lower prices. So this is a real choice, not a formality.

1. Hold your prices and rebuild margin

The low-risk option, and the one most businesses in fact take. It works because of how customers hold prices in their heads: they anchor on what they paid last time. A price that does not move is invisible — there is no new number to react to, no sign to put up, no conversation at the till. The 27p a cup goes quietly onto a margin that has been squeezed by wages, rates and green coffee prices for three years running. Suits you if your margin is at the thin end of that 3–15% range, or you have been putting off a price rise you know you need.

2. Pass it on in full, and make a noise about it

The opposite play: £3.60 becomes £3.30 and you tell everyone. Here the anchor works for you — “was £3.60, now £3.30” resets the reference price, and being visibly the café that dropped its prices in a month when nobody else did is worth more than 30p of goodwill. The risk is the one Ireland illustrates: cuts get reversed. A rate that comes back to 20% means putting your prices back up, and a price rise after a cut feels like a loss to customers in a way that the original price never did. Suits you if you are fighting a nearby chain on price, or a cut is announced as permanent rather than temporary.

3. Split it, and put your half where customers notice it

Take £3.60 to £3.45 and keep 13.6p. Honest, unremarkable, and it hedges the reversal risk — a 15p rise later is easier than a 30p one. The more interesting version is to keep the full 27p on the shelf price and spend a slice of it on the reward instead: a shorter card, a better free drink, a proper welcome stamp. A 15p price cut is genuinely invisible to a customer buying one coffee. “Your ninth coffee is free now, not your tenth” is something they can repeat to somebody else. It is the same money out of your pocket, landing in a place where it gets remembered. Suits you if you already have a loyalty scheme running and would rather buy repeat visits than a price position. Either way

How to talk to your regulars about it

Three short rules that hold whichever option you pick.

Do not promise anything before 28 October

“If VAT comes down we’ll pass it on” sounds generous in September and becomes a debt in November. Say nothing until there is something to say.

If you cut, say plainly why — and whether it lasts

“VAT on hospitality dropped, so our prices have too” is a good sign to have in the window. If the cut is temporary, say so at the time: “while the lower VAT rate lasts” costs you nothing now and saves the awkward conversation later. Announcing a cut as permanent and reversing it is the one version of this that damages trust.

If you hold, you do not owe anyone an explanation

Almost no customer tracks the VAT rate on their coffee, and nobody will ask. You are not concealing anything by leaving a price alone. If a regular does raise it, the honest answer is the true one: wages and rates went up more than this, and the money is keeping the doors open.

This week

Four things worth doing before Budget day

All of them are useful whether or not the rate moves, which is the point — none of this is a bet on the outcome.

1. Confirm whether you are registered
Five minutes

If your taxable turnover is under £90,000 and you are not registered, none of this applies to you and you can stop here. Worth knowing for certain rather than assuming.

2. Get your standard-rated share
One report

What percentage of last month’s takings was standard-rated? Multiply your annual VAT-inclusive takings by that share, then by 7.576%. That is the number a cut is worth to you if you hold prices — the only figure in this article that is actually about your café.

3. Decide now, not on the day
Hold / pass / split

Pick your option while you are calm and it is hypothetical. Budget day produces a lot of noise and some bad same-afternoon pricing decisions. If you decide in advance, 28 October is just an answer to a question you have already settled.

4. Know what your regulars are worth
Whatever happens

The rate is the Chancellor’s decision. How often your regulars come back is yours, and it moves more money than 27p a cup. If you cannot currently say how many of last month’s sales were repeat customers, that is the more valuable gap to close by 28 October.

Questions

Frequently asked

Is hospitality VAT being cut to 10%?

Not as of 28 September 2026. It is a campaign, not a policy. UKHospitality and more than 800 businesses have asked the government to cut the rate from 20% to 10%, and Andy Burnham backed the idea in February while he was mayor of Greater Manchester. Nothing has been announced. Chancellor John Healey delivers the Budget on Wednesday 28 October 2026, and that is the first realistic date for a decision either way.

Does VAT apply to takeaway coffee in the UK?

Yes, at the full 20%. Hot drinks are standard-rated whether the customer drinks them in or takes them away, so the eat-in and takeaway boundary makes no difference to a latte. It matters for food: cold takeaway food is normally zero-rated, while anything eaten on your premises is standard-rated. That is why two cafés with the same takings can have very different VAT bills, and why a rate cut would be worth more to a café where people sit in and eat.

If VAT is cut, do cafés have to lower prices?

No. There is no obligation to pass a VAT cut on, and most businesses historically do not pass on all of it. When Ireland cut its hospitality VAT rate from 13.5% to 9% in 2011, only about half the cut reached consumers as lower prices. Holding your prices is the low-risk option because customers anchor on the price they already pay, so an unchanged price is invisible to them while the saving goes straight to your margin.

How much would a 10% VAT rate save a small café?

If you keep your prices exactly as they are, a cut from 20% to 10% is worth 7.576% of your VAT-inclusive takings on the sales that move to the lower rate. On an illustrative £3.60 latte that is 27.3p a cup. A café selling 150 of those a day and holding its price would keep about £286 a week, or roughly £14,900 a year. At 300 a day it is about £573 a week, or £29,800 a year.

When is the 2026 Autumn Budget?

Wednesday 28 October 2026. Chancellor John Healey confirmed the date. The one hospitality tax change already confirmed ahead of it is a 20% cut to business rates bills for pubs, social clubs and live music venues in England from April 2027, which does not include cafés.

Would a VAT cut help a café that is not VAT-registered?

No, not at all. If your taxable turnover is below the £90,000 VAT registration threshold you are not charging VAT, so a lower rate changes nothing for you. Tax Policy Associates estimates that 45% of hospitality businesses would see no benefit from a hospitality VAT cut for exactly this reason. Check your position before you plan around it.

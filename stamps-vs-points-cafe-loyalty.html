Stamps or Points? Choosing the Right Loyalty Model for Your Café

Everyone tells you the chains run points and the independents run stamps. Look at what the biggest chain in Britain actually runs and that turns out not to be true — which is useful, because it means the question is not what is fashionable. It is what your basket mix can carry.

Last updated 29 September 2026 · UK independents · Worked examples are illustrative, not averages

?

Is a stamp card or a points system better for a coffee shop loyalty programme? For most independents, stamps. A stamp rewards a visit; a point rewards a pound. When nearly every transaction is about the same size — a drink-led café — the two are arithmetically identical, and the stamp wins because the customer can see how close they are without doing sums. Points earn their place when your transaction sizes vary widely, because a stamp scheme in a brunch-led venue quietly pays your cheapest customers several times the rate it pays your best ones.

The chains did not choose points either

This is the bit most comparison articles get wrong, and it changes how you should read the whole debate.

What Costa did in 2022
Dropped points

Costa replaced its points scheme with a free drink for every eight bought, plus a slice of cake on your birthday. The Drum reported that total transactions grew 16% after the relaunch, that over 70% of Costa Coffee Club transactions were by then made through the app, and that redemptions of free coffee accounted for 8% of all transactions.

What Costa runs today
Buy 10, get 1

Still a stamp card. Costa’s own page says it plainly: “Collect Beans with every coffee you buy — after 10, your next one’s on us.” One bean per drink, a fixed number for a reward. The word Beans makes it sound like a currency. The mechanic underneath is the eight-box card from the café on the corner, with two more boxes.

Who does run real points
Starbucks

Starbucks UK is the genuine points scheme: “Collect Stars based on how much you spend — for every £1, collect 10 Stars”, with rewards starting at 150 Stars and running to 1,150. Note what Starbucks sells that Costa sells less of: food, and bigger baskets. The mechanic follows the menu, not the marketing.

The lesson for you
Menu first

Two of the biggest coffee businesses in the country sat down with the same question and came to opposite answers, because they are selling different things. Nobody is going to hand you a rule that works for both. The rest of this page is how to work out which one you are.

One honest caveat on that 16%: it is a single company’s relaunch, reported in 2022, and the scheme changed alongside a heavy push on the app. It is the best UK café evidence in public, and it is still one data point, not a law.

The whole difference

A stamp rewards a visit. A point rewards a pound.

Everything else — fairness, cost, fraud, how easy it is to explain — falls out of that one sentence.

Why that is the same thing in a drink-led café

If nine transactions in ten are one coffee at £3.60, then a visit is a fixed number of pounds. A stamp scheme and a points scheme will hand back almost exactly the same money to almost exactly the same people. At that point the only thing left to choose on is which one the customer understands, and it is not close.

Why it stops being the same thing the moment you add brunch

Say a stamp is a stamp, whatever the transaction. Your £3.60 flat white regular earns a free drink after £36 of spend — a return of about 10%. Your £12 brunch regular earns the same free drink after £120 — a return of 3%. You are paying your cheapest customer more than three times the rate you pay your most valuable one, and neither of them can see it. A spend-based scheme returns the same percentage to both, which is what people mean when they say points are fairer.

And why that is not automatically an argument for points

Rewarding visits on purpose is a perfectly good strategy: frequency is the thing most independent cafés are actually short of, and a scheme that pays for the eleventh visit rather than the eleventh pound is aimed squarely at it. The mistake is not choosing stamps. The mistake is choosing stamps without knowing that is what you have chosen.

Why it works

Why customers finish stamp cards and abandon points balances

Two well-documented quirks of how people think, and one of them is the reason Starbucks has a 150-Star tier at all.

People speed up as they get close to a goal

This is the goal-gradient effect, and a stamp card is almost a perfect illustration of it. Eight empty boxes is an abstraction. Six filled boxes and two empty ones is an errand for Thursday. The card does the persuading for you, and it does it in the customer’s pocket rather than in your marketing. Points balances rarely produce that feeling, because the customer has no intuition for how far 380 is from 500.

Abstract currencies get discounted in the customer’s head

People are poor at valuing anything that is not in pounds, and they resolve the uncertainty by assuming the worst. A balance worth 38p feels like nothing; 380 Stars feels like something, which is precisely why chains rename their points. Notice that Starbucks does not stop at the rename: it shows rewards from 150 Stars up, so there is always a nearby goal, as well as tiers at 350, 600 and 1,150 for people who want to save. That is a points scheme buying back the one thing a stamp card gets for free.

What this means if you go with points anyway

Do the two things the chains do. Put your lowest reward close enough that a normal customer can reach it inside a fortnight, and show the balance as distance rather than as a number — “two more coffees”, not “380 points”. A points scheme that only ever shows a total is a stamp card with the boxes painted over.

Fifteen minutes with your till

Which suits your café? Three questions, answered from your own data

Pull a week of transactions — a normal week, not half term — and answer these in order. The first one settles it for most people.

1. What share of your transactions are a single drink?

Count transactions, not items. If more than roughly 70% of them are one drink and nothing else, stop here and run stamps: your visits and your pounds are the same measurement, and simplicity wins the tie. Between 40% and 70%, read on. Below 40% you are a food business that also sells coffee, and the next two questions matter more.

2. How wide is your spend per visit?

Find your median transaction, then the level that a tenth of your transactions sit above. Divide the second by the first. Under 2 and a stamp treats everyone roughly alike. Over 3 and a stamp scheme is quietly running a redistribution: it returns triple the percentage to your smallest baskets. At that spread, either move to points or split the scheme, which is the next section.

3. Do you need more visits, or bigger baskets?

These pull in opposite directions and you cannot have both from one mechanic. Stamps buy frequency: the reward arrives sooner the more often someone comes, whatever they spend. Points buy basket size: the reward arrives sooner the more they spend, however rarely they come. Independent cafés are usually short of frequency, which is the quiet reason stamps keep winning here. If your problem is that people come often and spend £3.60, points will not fix it either — that is a menu question, not a loyalty one.

If your answers disagree with each other, follow question one. A scheme that is technically fairer and that nobody joins returns nothing at all.

The middle option

The hybrid: stamps on coffee, spend-based on food

The right answer for a brunch-led venue, and the wrong answer for almost everyone else.

When it is genuinely right

You have two distinct customer behaviours in one room: a morning trade buying one coffee, and a weekend trade spending £25 on brunch for two. One mechanic cannot serve both without under-rewarding one of them. Run the stamp card on drinks, where it works, and a spend-based reward on food, where it works, and accept that you now have two things to explain.

One reward per mechanic. No tiers.

The temptation with a hybrid is to add levels — bronze, silver, a bonus on Tuesdays. Resist all of it. More options do not feel more generous to a customer standing in a queue; they feel like work, and the usual response to a decision that feels like work is to not make it. Two schemes, one reward each, described in two sentences. That is the ceiling.

The test that actually predicts failure

Hybrids rarely fail on the arithmetic. They fail because a part-time member of staff cannot explain the scheme during a morning rush, so they stop mentioning it, and a loyalty scheme nobody is offered is a loyalty scheme nobody joins. Before you launch, ask a new starter to describe it to you in fifteen seconds. If they cannot, that is your answer.

If you are changing

How to switch models without upsetting the people who already have a card

The people most annoyed by a change are the ones who were most loyal to the old scheme. They are also the ones you can least afford to lose.

Honour everything outstanding, and round in their favour

Publish the conversion before you switch, not after, and make it slightly generous. Six stamps becoming six stamps’ worth is the minimum; six stamps becoming seven costs you very little and buys the whole conversation. Set an end date for old cards far enough out that a normal customer will reach it without hurrying — eight to twelve weeks is usually plenty.

Never devalue quietly

Costa’s scheme was eight drinks for a free one in 2022 and is ten today. That is a 25% devaluation, it is reported to have happened in 2023, and customers still bring it up. A chain can absorb that. A café with four hundred regulars cannot. If you have to make the reward harder to reach, say so plainly, say why, and say it before it happens.

Change one thing at a time

Switching mechanic and moving from paper to a phone in the same week means that when something goes wrong — and something will — you will not know which change caused it. Move the mechanic first or the format first, give it a month, then do the other.

Questions

Frequently asked

Are stamp cards or points better for a café?

For most independent cafés, stamps. A stamp rewards a visit and a point rewards a pound, so the two are identical when almost every transaction is about the same size — which is the case in a drink-led café — and the stamp wins on simplicity because the customer can see how close they are without doing any arithmetic. Points earn their keep when your transaction sizes vary a lot, typically a brunch-led or food-heavy venue, because a stamp scheme there pays your cheapest customers several times the rate it pays your most valuable ones.

Did Costa switch from points to stamps?

In effect, yes, and it has never switched back. Costa replaced its old points scheme in 2022 with a buy-eight-get-one-free model plus a birthday treat, and The Drum reported that total transactions grew 16% after the relaunch. What Costa Club runs today is still a stamp card: its own site says you collect a Bean with every coffee and after ten the next one is free. The word Beans makes it sound like a currency, but one bean per drink and a fixed number for a reward is a stamp card by any other name.

How many points should a coffee be worth?

Work backwards from what you are willing to give away, not from a round number. Decide the percentage of takings you can afford to return — most café stamp schemes land somewhere around 8 to 11% — then set the reward threshold so the maths comes out there. At one point per pound and a £3.60 drink as the reward, 40 points returns 9% and 36 points returns 10%. Starbucks in the UK uses 10 Stars per £1 and redemption tiers from 150 Stars upwards, which is the same decision dressed in bigger numbers.

Can I use stamps and points together?

You can, and a brunch-led café is the case where it makes sense: stamps on the drinks, a spend-based scheme on the food. But run one reward per mechanic and no more. The most common way a hybrid fails is not the maths, it is that staff cannot explain it in one sentence at the till during a queue, so it stops being offered. If you cannot describe the whole scheme to a new starter in fifteen seconds, simplify it before you launch it.

Is a digital stamp card better than paper?

For the café, yes, mainly because of what it tells you. A paper card is cheap and everyone understands it, but it is anonymous, easy to forge with a borrowed stamp and it disappears with the customer’s wallet. A digital card on the phone lets you see how many people are halfway through a card, who has stopped coming and whether the scheme is actually changing behaviour — none of which paper can answer. The mechanic is identical either way, so switching from paper to digital is not the same decision as switching from stamps to points.

What about a coffee subscription instead?

A third option, and a different question: a subscription sells frequency up front rather than rewarding it afterwards, which changes your cash flow as much as your marketing. It suits a café with a dependable weekday trade and enough capacity to absorb heavier users. We have compared it with a stamp card in the subscription piece. And if your worry is specifically that younger customers ignore the card, the data on Gen Z and loyalty is not what most people expect.

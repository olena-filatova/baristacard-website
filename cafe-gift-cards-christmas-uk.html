The Christmas gift card opportunity independent cafés are missing

Every December your regulars go looking for presents for people who would love your café, and most small cafés give them nothing to buy. A gift card fixes that. This guide covers what it actually does for your cash, what HMRC and consumer law say, what physical and digital cards cost, how to turn a card into a regular, and a four-week plan to be ready by December.

Last updated 17 September 2026 · UK · Sources: HMRC, legislation.gov.uk, ONS, the Gift Card & Voucher Association, Which?

?

Should my independent café sell Christmas gift cards? Yes, if you can set it up by the end of November and you treat the money properly. A gift card lets people who already like your café buy it as a present, so the cash arrives in December and the visits arrive in January and February, when trade is usually quieter. Most of those visits come from someone who was given the card, which makes it one of the few ways a small café gets a new customer introduced by a friend. Until a card is spent, the money is still owed to the holder, so do not spend it as profit, and check the VAT treatment with your accountant, because for most cafés VAT is due when the card is used, not when it is sold.

Why now

Why gift cards matter more in December

The case is not that December is quiet. For most cafés it is not. The case is that December is when other people are looking for a reason to spend money on you.

Your regulars are already shopping
Demand you are not catching

Think about the woman who comes in every Tuesday with her laptop. In December she is buying presents for a sister, a colleague, a neighbour. Some of those people live near you. If you sold a gift card, some of those presents would be your café. If you do not, the money goes to a chain voucher or a candle.

Gift cards are not a niche habit. The Gift Card & Voucher Association’s monthly tracker, run by GlobalData across 2,000 UK shoppers, found that 17.4% had bought a gift card for someone else in June 2025, which is not even a big gifting month. In the same survey, 31.1% agreed they have bought or will buy gift cards to support companies they like. For an independent café, that second number is the one that matters: people want to back the places they are fond of, and a gift card is an easy way to do it.

It is a present that feels personal
Not a generic voucher

“This is the place I go every morning, you should try it” is a better present than a high-street voucher, and the buyer knows it. That is your pitch to them, and you do not have to invent it. You are selling them a thoughtful present that happens to cost the same as a box of chocolates.

The honest timing argument
Sell in December, serve in January

You will sometimes read that gift cards rescue a café’s slowest month. December is rarely that month. The nearest monthly figures the Office for National Statistics publishes are for pubs and bars rather than cafés, but the shape is clear: in 2019, the last full year before lockdowns, December was the highest month and January the lowest. Turnover fell by about 24% from December 2019 to January 2020, and by a similar amount from December 2021 to January 2022.

So the real opportunity is a shift in time. You take the money during the weeks when your till is already busy, and a good share of the visits land in the weeks after New Year, when people have stopped going out and you have spare tables. A gift card is one of the few things that fills January without a discount.

The money

The cash-flow case: money in now, redeemed later

A gift card is paid for today and spent some time later. That is useful, as long as you remember whose money it is.

Cash you hold before you serve
Paid up front

Sell forty £20 cards across December and you have £800 in the bank before a single extra coffee has been made. That can smooth a January when rent, a quarterly bill and a quiet till all land together. It is the same logic as selling prepaid drinks or a coffee subscription, which we compared in stamp card or coffee subscription. The difference is that the person who paid is usually not the person who will visit.

It is not income yet
A promise you still owe

Until a card is spent, that £800 is a promise to serve £800 of food and drink. If you treat it as December profit and spend it, January arrives with the same bills plus forty people expecting to be fed. Keep a running total of the balance still unspent, keep enough cash to cover it, and ask your accountant how they want it recorded in your books. Cards that are never fully spent are a real effect, but do not plan your budget around them.

VAT: probably due when the card is spent
The rule most owners miss

Since 1 January 2019, HMRC splits vouchers into two kinds. A single-purpose voucher is one where, when it is sold, the VAT rate of everything it can buy is already known; VAT is due at the moment of sale. A multi-purpose voucher is any other voucher; the sale itself is ignored for VAT and the tax is due only when the card is used to buy something.

A café usually sells things at more than one rate. HMRC’s catering guidance says hot takeaway drinks are standard-rated while cold takeaway food is generally zero-rated, and food eaten on the premises is standard-rated. A card that can be spent on anything in your café therefore does not have a known VAT rate when it is sold, which points to a multi-purpose voucher. HMRC also says that if a multi-purpose voucher is never used, there is no VAT to account for on it.

If your café only sells standard-rated items, or your card can only be spent on hot drinks, the answer may be different. That is a conversation with your accountant before you sell the first card, not after.

A gift card and a prepaid bundle are not the same thing
Check which one you are selling

HMRC’s definition of a voucher includes being transferable by gift, and it lists non-transferable vouchers among the things that are not vouchers for these rules. A £20 card anyone can spend is a voucher. A bundle of ten coffees tied to one customer’s own loyalty account may not be. Both are useful. They are not taxed the same way, so tell your accountant which one you are selling.

The part people overlook

“Someone else brought them in”: a new-customer channel

The buyer is already yours. The person holding the card usually is not. That is the real prize.

A recommendation with money attached
The warmest introduction

Most ways of finding new customers cost you money and reach strangers. A gift card reaches a stranger who has been personally recommended to you by someone they like, and who has already been paid to try you. There is no advert that does that.

It also sets up a small sense of obligation in the right direction. The recipient was given something, and it is natural to want to use it properly and say thank you. People tend to repay a kindness; that instinct is part of why a present from a friend gets used when a discount code from a brand does not.

The first visit decides everything
Make it easy to come back

A gift card visit is a trial run. If the recipient spends the card and never returns, you have sold £20 of coffee at full price and gained nothing else. If they come back three more times, you have a new regular. Brief your team to notice gift card redemptions, to be warm about it (“someone likes you”), and to offer the loyalty card on the spot. That thirty-second moment is where the value is made.

If they do drift away in February, the same rules apply as for any lapsed customer. We set out what to do in why your regulars stop coming in.

Leave a little over
A small nudge to return

A £20 card rarely gets spent in one go in a café. That is fine. A small leftover balance is a reason to come back, and the second visit is where a habit starts. Make sure your till shows the remaining balance on the receipt, so the holder knows it is there.

Format

Physical vs digital for a small café

Different buyers want different things. If your till system does both, offer both.

Physical card

A cost per card

Something to hold, wrap or tuck into a Christmas card. A small stack by the till sells itself to people already queuing. You have to order stock in advance, and you pay for cards that do not sell.

Digital card

No stock to buy

Bought online, sent by email, and still possible on Christmas Eve. Reaches the regular’s brother three counties away. Less of a moment when it is opened, so make the email look like a present.

Our suggestion

Both, from one system

One balance, two formats

Use the gift card feature in the till you already have, so both formats share one balance and your staff learn one process. Order a modest first batch of physical cards and let digital take the late rush.

Keeping them

Pairing a gift card with your loyalty scheme

The gift card gets them through the door once. The loyalty card is what brings them back.

Give them a head start
Endowed progress

People are more likely to finish something they feel they have already started. In a well-known study published in the Journal of Consumer Research in 2006, Joseph Nunes and Xavier Drèze gave car-wash customers loyalty cards that all needed eight more washes. One group had an eight-stamp card; the other had a ten-stamp card with two stamps already filled in. About 34% of the head-start group earned their reward, against 19% of the others.

The café version is simple. When someone spends a gift card, sign them up to your loyalty card and add a welcome stamp or two. They leave already on their way to a free drink, which is a better reason to come back than a leftover balance alone. Before you do it, work out what those free stamps really cost you; we did the sums in loyalty reward maths.

Put the card on their phone, not in a drawer
Wallet, not paper

A paper stamp card given to a first-time visitor usually ends up in a coat pocket. A loyalty card saved to Apple or Google Wallet sits on the phone they carry everywhere, updates the moment a stamp is added, and needs no app. For a gift card recipient who has not yet decided whether you are “their” café, that is the difference between being remembered and being forgotten.

Tell your regulars you sell them
They are your buyers

The people most likely to buy your gift cards are already on your loyalty scheme. A short message in late November (“Our gift cards are here, perfect for anyone who’s heard you talk about us”) reaches exactly the right people. If you have their email with permission, one short email does the same job; our guide to wallet pass, SMS or email covers which channel suits which message.

Prepaid bundles are a different tool
For regulars buying for themselves

Some regulars would rather buy ahead for themselves: ten coffees now, drunk over the month. That is a prepaid bundle rather than a gift card, and it lives naturally on the loyalty card rather than at the till. It is worth offering alongside gift cards, but keep the two separate in your own head, and in your accountant’s.

Staying on the right side

Terms that keep you out of trouble

Short, plain and printed where the buyer will see them. That is most of it.

Expiry dates are allowed, not required
No legal minimum

UK law does not require a gift card to have an expiry date, and it does not set a minimum period. What it does require is fairness. Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, an unfair term in a consumer contract is not binding on the consumer, and written terms must be transparent: in plain, intelligible language and legible. Which?’s consumer guidance says expiry dates are allowed as long as the buyer was told about them at the time of purchase.

In practice: if you set an expiry, print it on the card, show it on the website before payment, and put it on the receipt. Pick a period long enough that a present opened on Christmas Day can comfortably be used. Some providers, Square among them, simply issue cards that do not expire, which removes the question entirely.

Five lines to write down
Before you sell one

1. Where it can be used (this café only, or all your sites). 2. What it can be spent on (everything, or drinks only; this also affects VAT). 3. The expiry date, if any. 4. Whether a remaining balance stays on the card. 5. What happens if it is lost, and whether it can be cancelled if you still have the details. Keep each to one sentence.

Keep a proper record
Your till should do this

You need to know, at any moment, how much has been sold and how much is still unspent. A proper gift card system tracks this for you. A book of handwritten paper vouchers does not, and it is also easy to copy. For anything beyond a handful of cards, use the system built into your till.

The plan

A simple four-week countdown to be ready for December

Start in the first week of November and you are selling by the time people start their Christmas shopping. Each step fits into a quiet afternoon.

Week 1: decide and switch on
Early November

Check whether your till provider has gift cards built in and what it charges. Speak to your accountant about the VAT treatment and how to record unspent balances. Choose one or two card values; £10 and £20 cover most café presents. Write your five lines of terms. Switch on digital cards first, because they need no stock.

Week 2: cards, counter and team
Mid November

Order a modest batch of physical cards; you can reorder, but you cannot un-order. Make a small sign for the counter. Show every member of staff how to sell a card, how to take one as payment, and how to check a balance. Agree what the barista does when a gift card is spent: a warm word, the loyalty card, a welcome stamp.

Week 3: tell the people who will buy
Late November

Send one message to your loyalty members and, if you have permission, one email to your list. Post the link to your digital cards on your social accounts and your website. Mention it at the till when a regular pays; a single sentence is enough. Buy one yourself and send it to a friend, so you see exactly what a customer receives.

Week 4: into December
Late November to Christmas Eve

Keep the cards visible at the till all month. In the last week before Christmas, remind people that digital cards can still be sent on the day. Keep a note of how many cards you sell and the total unspent balance, and set that cash aside.

And then: January
Where the value is made

This is when the cards come back. Make sure every recipient leaves with your loyalty card on their phone. At the end of February, look at how many of them have been back a second time. That number, not the December sales total, tells you whether to do it again next year.

Questions

Frequently asked

Should my independent café sell Christmas gift cards?

Yes, if you can set it up by the end of November and you treat the money properly. A gift card lets people who already like your café buy it as a present, so the cash arrives in December and the visits arrive in January and February, when trade is usually quieter. Most of those visits come from someone who was given the card, which makes it one of the few ways a small café gets a new customer introduced by a friend. Until a card is spent, the money is still owed to the holder, so do not spend it as profit, and check the VAT treatment with your accountant, because for most cafés VAT is due when the card is used rather than when it is sold.

Do gift cards work for small independent cafés?

They can, because the people buying them are your regulars and they are choosing a present they already trust. In a GlobalData survey of 2,000 UK shoppers for the Gift Card and Voucher Association, 17.4% said they had bought a gift card for someone else in June 2025, and 31.1% agreed they have bought or will buy gift cards to support companies they like. A small café does not need a large scheme: a single card value or two, sold at the till and online, with plain terms, is enough to start. The work that matters is getting each recipient to come back a second time.

Should a café offer physical or digital gift cards?

Offer both if your till provider supports both, because they suit different buyers. A physical card is something to hand over or put in a Christmas card, and it sells itself from a display by the till, but it costs money per card and has to be ordered in advance. A digital card costs nothing to stock, can be bought at eleven at night by someone who lives in another city, and can still be sent on Christmas Eve. On 17 September 2026 Square listed digital cards as free to set up and physical cards from 52p each, and SumUp listed no monthly cost for its gift cards.

When do I pay VAT on a café gift card?

It depends on whether HMRC would treat your card as a single-purpose or a multi-purpose voucher. A single-purpose voucher is one where the VAT rate of everything it can buy is known when it is sold, and VAT is due at the point of sale. A multi-purpose voucher is any other voucher, and VAT is due only when it is spent. Because a café usually sells some things at the standard rate, such as hot drinks, and some at zero rate, such as eligible cold takeaway food, a card that can be spent on anything in the café is likely to be multi-purpose. If a multi-purpose voucher is never used, there is no VAT to pay on it. Confirm your own case with your accountant.

Do café gift cards have to have an expiry date?

No. UK law does not require an expiry date and does not set a minimum period. You can set one, but the Consumer Rights Act 2015 says unfair terms do not bind the consumer and written terms must be in plain, intelligible language, and Which? advises that expiry dates are allowed as long as the buyer was told about them when they bought the card. Print the expiry date clearly on the card and on the receipt, and choose a period long enough that nobody could reasonably feel caught out.

Café loyalty ROI calculator

Is a loyalty app worth it for a small café? The monthly fee turns out to be the smallest number in the answer. Here is the sum that actually decides it, and a calculator you can fill in with your own prices.

Last updated 11 September 2026 · VAT per HMRC Notice 709/1 · Staff time at the April 2026 National Living Wage

Is BaristaCard worth it for a small café — what's the ROI? For a single site the software is not what decides it: the Starter plan is free for your first venue, and the £9 plan is covered by fewer than four extra coffees a month. What decides it is whether your loyalty scheme brings regulars in more often than its rewards cost. On a free-after-9 card, that bar is about 14% more visits from members — roughly one extra visit for every seven they already make. Clear it and the scheme pays; fall short and you are handing a discount to people who were coming anyway, whatever app you use.

Before you calculate

The three numbers you need

You know all three already, or can find them in ten minutes. A fourth number — how much more often people will come — nobody knows in advance, which is why the calculator lets you try several.

1. What a drink is worth to you
£2.83 in, £2.53 kept

Not the price on the board. A hot takeaway drink carries 20% VAT, so a £3.40 flat white is £2.83 in your business. Take off roughly 30p of coffee, milk and cup and each paid cup leaves £2.53. The first figure is what a free drink costs you; the second is what an extra visit earns you. We worked both out properly in loyalty reward maths.

2. How often your regulars come now
Twice a week = 8.7 a month

This matters more than it looks, because every reward you give a regular who was already coming is pure cost. The more often your members visit today, the more you pay out before the scheme changes anything. It is the number that makes loyalty expensive, and the one most calculators leave out.

3. How generous your card is
Free after 9 = 1 in 10

Free after 9 means one visit in ten is free, so rewards cost about 10% of what members spend. Free after 5 is one in six — nearly 17%. This single choice sets the height of the bar the scheme has to clear.

Read across a row, not down
£29 moves the bar by 1%

Going from the free plan to the most expensive one changes the result by £29 a month. Going from 10% to 20% more visits changes it by £290. The plan you pick barely matters; whether the scheme changes behaviour matters ten times more. That is why comparing loyalty apps on monthly fee alone is comparing the wrong number.

The top row is the real risk
−£391 a month

If nobody changes their habits, the café hands out 130 free flat whites a month to people who would have paid for them — £368 — plus about £23 of staff time at the till. That loss has nothing to do with the software, and it is the same on every app, including a free one. It is the cost of a scheme that rewards loyalty without creating any.

The bar is a percentage
Same for weekly or daily

A regular who comes every weekday needs about 3.5 extra visits a month to cover their rewards; a twice-a-week regular needs about 1.2. Different numbers, same share: roughly 13.5% more visits in both cases. So you can judge the scheme with one figure across all your regulars, however often each one comes in.

In practice

How long payback actually takes

With no set-up fee there is nothing up front to earn back. The question is really how long before you can tell whether it is working.

Month one flatters you
First reward in week five

On free after 9, a twice-a-week regular reaches their free drink on their tenth visit — about five weeks in. So for the first month you collect visits and pay out almost nothing, and the scheme looks brilliant. Do not judge it then.

Month three tells the truth
Compare like with like

By then rewards are flowing at their normal rate. Compare how often members visit now with how often they came before they joined. If you ran a paper card or kept any record before, use it; if not, note your busiest regulars' habits in the fortnight before launch. Our guide to what a loyalty app really costs covers the staff-time side of the same check.

Help people finish the card
The endowed progress effect

In a well-known loyalty-card experiment, researchers gave customers either an 8-stamp card or a 10-stamp card with two stamps already filled in. Both needed eight purchases. The head-start card was completed more often, and faster (Nunes & Drèze, Journal of Consumer Research, 2006). A card someone is already part-way through feels like something to finish rather than something to start — and finishing is where the extra visits come from.

If your system lets you give new members a stamp or two when they join, that is the cheapest way we know to push the percentage in the table above upwards.

If the numbers don't stack up yet

What to change first

In order of how much they move the answer.

Make the reward cheaper, not rarer
Biggest lever

Rewards are the largest cost by far, so this is where the money is. Naming a filter coffee instead of any drink, or capping the reward at a set value, cuts the cost without taking stamps away — which customers notice and resent. The sums are in loyalty reward maths.

Recruit the occasional customers
Where the extra visits live

Your daily regular has little room to come more often. Someone who drops in once a fortnight has plenty. A scheme that only signs up the people already at the counter every morning will struggle to clear 14%. Offer the card to the customers you see least, not most.

Keep the till quick
£23 a month at 5 seconds

In the example, five seconds a scan costs about £23 a month; fifteen seconds would cost about £69. If a scheme slows the queue in the morning rush, that time comes straight off the result above.

Stay on the free plan until it proves itself
No tie-in

A single café can run BaristaCard on the Starter plan for nothing and move up only once the numbers justify it. If after three months your members are not coming in more often, you have learned that cheaply.

Questions

Frequently asked

How do I calculate ROI for a café loyalty app?

Compare what the scheme gives away with what it brings back. Every reward costs you the net menu price of the drink — £2.83 on a £3.40 flat white once VAT is taken off — because the customer would otherwise have paid. Every extra visit the scheme causes brings in that price less the cost of the drink, about £2.53. Add the software fee and a few seconds of staff time per scan, and the scheme pays for itself once members visit roughly 14% more often than they did before. On free after 9, that break-even barely changes with the software plan: 13.5% on a free plan, 14.5% on £29 a month.

How long does it take a loyalty app to pay for itself?

With no set-up fee there is nothing up front to pay back, so the real question is how long before you can tell whether the scheme is working. Allow three months. The first month flatters you, because on free after 9 a twice-a-week regular does not earn a reward until week five, so costs lag behind sign-ups. By month three rewards are flowing at their normal rate and you can compare members' visit frequency with what it was before launch.

Is BaristaCard worth it for a small café — what's the ROI?

For a single-site café the software fee is not what decides it: BaristaCard's Starter plan is free for one venue, and the £9 Basic plan is covered by fewer than four extra coffees a month. What decides the ROI is the scheme itself. In a worked example of 150 regulars visiting twice a week on free after 9, the scheme costs about £391 a month if nobody changes their habits, and makes about £189 a month if members come in 20% more often. Break-even sits at about 13.5% more visits.

How to Get Your Café Recommended by Google Maps and AI Assistants

Two separate rulebooks govern how you are allowed to ask for a review, and they do not draw the line in the same place. UK law bans hiding an incentive; Google bans the incentive. Almost everything written for café owners covers the first and misses the second — which matters, because the second is the one that can actually cost you something.

Last updated 30 September 2026 · UK independents · General business information, not legal advice

?

How do I get my café recommended on Google Maps and by AI assistants like ChatGPT? Both work from public text about your café, most of which is your Google reviews. Google ranks you on relevance, distance and prominence, and counts reviews towards prominence — so quantity helps you appear. But an assistant answering “somewhere quiet to work near here” has to find a sentence that mentions working quietly, so specificity is what gets you named. You cannot ask customers to say specific things — Google forbids it — so you give them specific things worth mentioning, make reviewing one tap, ask after they have left, and reply to everything.

Start here

There are two rulebooks, and the strict one is not the law

If you have read anything about this, you have read about the fake-review law. That is the half that is unlikely to affect you. The half that will is Google’s own policy.

UK law
Bans the concealment

Since April 2025, Schedule 20 of the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 lists as a banned practice “submitting, or commissioning another person to submit or write—a fake consumer review, or a consumer review that conceals the fact it has been incentivised”, and publishing reviews “in a misleading way”.

Read it closely: the prohibited thing is a review that hides the incentive. The Competition and Markets Authority can fine a business up to 10% of its global turnover for infringing consumer law — a ceiling designed for large companies, and one the CMA is not realistically going to apply to a twelve-seat coffee shop. Its published fake-review investigations have been aimed at platforms and large sellers.

Google’s policy
Bans the incentive

Google’s Maps user-contributed content policy lists, under prohibited merchant practices, offering “incentives – such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services – in exchange for posting any review or revision or removal of a negative review.”

There is no disclosure that fixes this. A free coffee is a free good. Disclose it honestly and you are inside the law and still outside Google’s policy. And Google’s enforcement is automated, aimed at businesses of exactly your size, and lands on the thing you depend on: it can stop your profile receiving new reviews, unpublish the reviews you already have, or display a public notice on your profile saying fake reviews were removed.

What this means for you
One rule, not two

You do not need to hold two standards in your head. Google’s is stricter on every point that matters to a café, so follow Google’s and you satisfy both. Everything in the rest of this article is written to that line.

And the good news is in the same policy, in the sentence everyone skips. Google explicitly permits merchants to “solicit or encourage the posting of content that does represent a genuine experience, without offering incentives to do so or attempting to influence the rating or the contents of the review.” Asking is allowed. Asking is, in fact, the whole strategy.

The mechanism

Ranking and being recommended are two different jobs

They need different things from your reviews, which is why cafés with plenty of reviews still never get named.

Google Maps ranks on three things
Reviews feed one of them

Google names them plainly: relevance (“how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for”), distance (“how far each business is from the customer who’s searching”) and prominence (“how well-known a business is”). Prominence is partly review-driven: Google says it is based on information including how many reviews you have, and that “more reviews and positive ratings can help your business’s local ranking.”

Distance you cannot change. Relevance is mostly your profile fields — categories, services, attributes. Prominence is where review count does real work. So for ranking, more genuine reviews is simply good, and that is the boring answer.

An assistant has to find a sentence
Count does not help

When someone asks an assistant for “a good flat white near London Bridge” or “somewhere quiet to work with plugs”, the system is matching that question against text. It can only name you if something written about you addresses it.

This is the part that changes what you do. Two hundred reviews saying “lovely place, great staff” are worth almost nothing here. They are interchangeable with every other café’s two hundred. Fifty reviews that mention the single-origin guest espresso, the quiet back room, the dog bowl by the door and the fact you open at seven will get you named in answers the other café never appears in — because those are the questions people actually ask.

The awkward constraint
And the way round it

The obvious move — ask customers to mention the guest espresso — is banned. Google’s policy says merchants should not “request that specific content be included”, and specifically calls out asking staff to solicit reviews containing particular content.

So the lever is not the ask. It is the visit. People describe what stood out, and nothing else. If you want reviews that mention a thing, make the thing noticeable: name the guest coffee on a card at the till, keep one table free for laptops and say so, put the water bowl where people trip over it. That is not a marketing tactic dressed up as one — it is the only compliant route to specific reviews there is.

What you can and cannot do

What to actually do

Four steps, none of which break a rule

This is the whole method. It is slower than buying reviews and it is the only version that survives contact with either rulebook.

Ask after they have left
Not at the counter

Google’s policy is precise here: merchants “should not require or pressure users to leave ratings or write reviews while on the premises”. Note the words. A passive prompt a customer can walk past — a card by the till, a QR on the table — is not pressure. A barista holding a tablet and waiting is.

There is a better reason to wait, though, and it is about how people remember things. We judge an experience by its best moment and by how it ended, and at the till the visit has not ended yet — they are queuing, holding a bag, thinking about the change. The review you want is the one written twenty minutes later on the bus, when the whole visit has resolved into a feeling. So the ask should arrive then: in a message, not in a conversation. Which channel works best for a café is a question in itself, and we have compared Wallet passes, SMS and email on exactly that.

Make it one tap
Every step loses people

Get the short review link from your own Google Business Profile — Google generates one for you — and use that single link everywhere. Not “search for us on Google and scroll down”. Not a homepage that then links onwards.

Then put it where you already have permission to reach people: a message to your loyalty members, an email to anyone who asked for one, a small card they take away with them. Anyone already carrying your loyalty card has made a small commitment to you once already, and a second small step after a first one is a far easier ask than a cold one. If you are not sure your sign-up is capturing those people in the first place, that is a separate and fixable problem. And if you are messaging customers at all, make sure the permission behind it is sound — what UK data rules ask of a café loyalty scheme is shorter than you fear, but it is not nothing.

Give them something to notice
Because you cannot ask

You are not allowed to request specific content, so the specificity has to come from the visit. Pick three things you want to be known for and make each of them impossible to miss. A named guest coffee on a card. The back table kept clear with a small sign saying it is for laptops. Opening times on the door that say 7am in a street where everyone else says nine.

Then read what comes back. Reviews are the only free, unfiltered record of which of your decisions customers actually registered. If you changed the milk and nobody mentions it, nobody noticed. That is worth knowing on its own, before any of this touches search.

Reply to everything
Especially the bad ones

Replies are the only text on your profile you write yourself, so they are worth using properly. Keep each one short, name the specific thing, say what changed, stop. Do not argue, do not explain at length, and never offer anything to get a review altered or removed — that is explicitly inside Google’s prohibition.

You are not writing to the reviewer. You are writing to the next person who reads the page, and a calm, specific reply to a two-star review reassures them more than the two-star review worries them. A profile where the owner answers everything reads as a real business paying attention. A profile with forty reviews and no replies reads as an empty room.

Twenty minutes, once

Your Google Business Profile checklist for 2026

This is the relevance half, and unlike reviews it is entirely under your control. Most cafés have four or five of these wrong.

  • Primary category. “Coffee shop” and “Café” are different categories and rank differently. Pick the one that matches what you mostly sell, then add the other as secondary.
  • Secondary categories. Brunch restaurant, bakery, breakfast restaurant, sandwich shop — add what is true. Each one is a query you become eligible for.
  • Opening hours, and the ones that differ. Add special hours for bank holidays now, while you are thinking about it.
  • The clocks go back on Sunday 25 October 2026. If you open on clock time rather than by the light, check the Sunday hours you have listed still describe what you do.
  • Menu. Add it as structured items with prices, not as a photograph of a chalkboard. Text can be matched to a question; a photograph cannot.
  • Attributes. Dog-friendly, free wi-fi, outdoor seating, step-free access, vegan options, good for working. These are exactly the filters people search with.
  • Photos, dated this year. The inside, the counter, the front from the pavement so people recognise it, and the food as it actually looks.
  • Products or services. List the drinks by name. This is a free place to put the words “oat flat white” and “single origin” in Google’s own index of you.
  • Description. 750 characters. Say what you are, where you are and what makes you different, in plain sentences.
  • Your website link, working and current. Broken links on a profile are commoner than you would think.

Nothing here is a growth hack. It is the equivalent of putting a sign above the door, and a surprising number of cafés have not.

Five minutes

Find out what AI already says about you

Do this before you change anything, so you have something to compare against in a month.

Ask the question a customer would ask

Open ChatGPT, Google’s Gemini and Perplexity in turn and type the same thing into each: “best independent coffee near [your street or nearest landmark]”. Then ask the sharper version — “where can I work quietly with a laptop near [area]”, “dog-friendly coffee near [area]”, “where’s open early near [area]”. Write down, for each, whether you were named.

Read what it says about you, not just whether it mentions you

If you are named, look at why. The reason it gives you is a direct readout of which of your review sentences are doing the work. If it says something wrong — the old opening hours, a menu you dropped — that tells you which public source is stale, and usually it is your own profile.

Treat the answers as weather, not verdicts

These systems give different answers to the same question on different days, and to different people. One run proves very little. The pattern across several questions and a few weeks is the signal: whether you appear at all for the specific questions, and whether the reasons given get more accurate as your profile and reviews improve.

What not to do about it

There is a small industry offering to get you into AI answers. Almost all of it resolves to the same two things this article describes — an accurate profile and genuine, specific reviews — or to buying reviews, which puts your profile at risk of a public warning notice. There is no third mechanism, because there is nothing else for these systems to read.

Questions

Frequently asked

Is it legal to give customers a free coffee for leaving a review in the UK?

The banned practice in UK law is the concealment, not the incentive itself: a review that hides the fact it was incentivised is prohibited, and the CMA can fine a business up to 10% of its global turnover. But a disclosed incentive still breaks Google’s Maps policy, which bans offering free goods in exchange for a review outright — and Google’s penalties land on your profile, up to a public notice saying fake reviews were removed. For an independent café that is the sharper risk of the two. So: don’t. This is general business information, not legal advice; if you have a specific worry, take proper advice.

Can I give a free coffee for a Google review in the UK?

No, if you want to stay inside Google’s rules, and there is no version of it that disclosure rescues. A free coffee is a free good, and Google prohibits offering free goods in exchange for posting any review. The distinction people miss is that being lawful and being within Google’s policy are two different tests, and Google is the one hosting your reviews. If you want to reward a customer for bringing you a new customer, that is a genuinely different mechanic with a different rulebook — we have set out how referrals and review rewards compare, and why only one of them is available to you.

How many Google reviews does a café need?

There is no threshold, and a target number is the wrong goal. Review count does feed Google’s prominence factor, so more genuine reviews helps you rank. But being ranked and being recommended are different jobs: an assistant answering a specific question needs a review that addresses it. Fifty specific reviews beat two hundred that say “lovely”. A steady trickle also beats a sudden burst, which is what manipulation looks like from the outside.

Do AI assistants use Google reviews?

For local recommendations they lean on the same public material Google Maps surfaces — your Business Profile and its reviews, your own site, any directory or press mention. Nobody outside these companies knows the exact recipe and it changes often. What is safe to build on is the shape: they answer by finding text that matches the question, so you are recommendable to the extent that public text about you says specific, matchable things.

How do I reply to a bad review?

Once, briefly, in public, without arguing. Thank them, name what went wrong, say what you have changed, stop. You are writing for the next reader, not the reviewer. And never offer anything to get it revised or removed — Google’s prohibition covers incentives for the revision or removal of a negative review as explicitly as it covers incentives for posting one.

What counts as a fake review under UK law?

Schedule 20 of the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 lists submitting or commissioning a fake review, or one that conceals having been incentivised, and publishing reviews in a misleading way. A fake review is one that presents itself as genuine but is not based on a real experience — which is why a review written by you, your family or your staff is the clearest breach there is, whatever it happens to say about the coffee.

Someone has left an obviously false review. What can I do?

Report it through your Business Profile rather than replying angrily, and say specifically why it is not a genuine customer experience. Then reply in public, briefly and neutrally, noting that you have no record of the visit and inviting them to contact you — that reply is for everyone else reading. Removals are not quick and not guaranteed. The durable defence is volume: one bad review among sixty recent ones reads as an outlier, and among six it reads as a pattern.

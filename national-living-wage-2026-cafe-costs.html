The April 2026 wage rise costs a small café about £3,000 a year. Here’s where to find it

The National Living Wage went up 50p an hour in April, and the rate for 18 to 20 year olds went up 85p. We worked through what that really adds to a small café’s staff bill once National Insurance and pensions are counted — and it is not always the number you have been told. Then: why a price rise alone is a fragile fix, and three places independents can find the money instead.

Last updated 15 September 2026 · Applies across the UK · Figures from GOV.UK, HMRC and The Pensions Regulator

£

How much does the 2026 National Living Wage rise cost my café? From 1 April 2026 the National Living Wage is £12.71 an hour for staff aged 21 and over, and £10.85 for 18 to 20 year olds. For three full-time staff aged 22 or over on the legal minimum, that is £2,925 a year in extra wages, about £3,013 once pension contributions are added. Employer National Insurance would add another £439 — but if you claim the £10,500 Employment Allowance, most small cafés pay no employer National Insurance at all. Nothing about National Insurance itself changed this April.

The youth rate rose fastest

The 18 to 20 rate went up twice as fast as the adult rate. UKHospitality called it “higher than expected” and estimated the April rises add £1.4bn to the sector’s costs. The gap between the two rates is closing, so if your rota leans on younger staff, expect that cost to keep climbing faster than the rest.

One thing to put in the diary: the day a 20-year-old barista turns 21, their minimum jumps from £10.85 to £12.71 — £1.86 an hour, or about £1,934 a year for someone on 20 hours a week.

National Insurance did not change
Same as April 2025

The big National Insurance change was a year earlier. Since April 2025 employers pay 15% on each employee’s earnings above £5,000 a year, and the Employment Allowance, which knocks up to £10,500 off your employer National Insurance bill, is open to employers of any size. All three figures are unchanged for 2026/27. What has changed is that higher wages mean a bit more National Insurance on each pound — which only costs you money if your bill is bigger than the allowance.

Two quiet exemptions
Under 21 · under 22

Employer National Insurance is 0% for staff under 21 on earnings up to £50,270 a year. And you only have to automatically enrol staff into a workplace pension if they are aged 22 or over and earn at least £10,000 a year; your minimum contribution is then 3% of their earnings above £6,240. So an 18 to 20 year old’s pay rise costs you exactly the pay rise — nothing on top.

The obvious answer

Why “just put prices up” doesn’t fully cover it

On paper, the price rise needed is tiny. That is exactly what makes it fragile.

The arithmetic
About 7p a sale

Café one’s £3,013 is £57.94 a week. If it sells 1,000 items a week, it needs to keep an extra 5.8p on each one. Hot drinks carry 20% VAT, so that is roughly 7p on the menu price. Easy, it seems.

The catch
Lose 22 cups, lose the gain

That 7p only covers the bill if every single sale still happens. A £3.40 flat white leaves about £2.53 after VAT and ingredients. Lose around 22 cups a week — about one sale in 45 — and the price rise has earned you nothing. The customers most likely to notice are your most frequent ones, who pay the rise five times a week.

And it isn’t the only rise
Rates, stock, next April

Many cafés in England also lost their 40% hospitality rates discount this April — see what changed with café business rates in 2026. If you pay a supervisor above the minimum and want to keep the gap, their pay moves too. And the minimum wage is reviewed again every year. Pricing is part of the answer; it works better when it isn’t carrying the whole load.

Options, not rules

Where independents can find the margin

Three places worth checking before, or alongside, a price rise. None needs new equipment.

Rota against the till
1 hour a weekday ≈ £3,300

Export a month of sales by hour from your till and lay it next to your rota. The question is simple: which hours have two people on and the sales of one? One fewer staffed hour each weekday, at £12.71, is £3,305 a year in wages — on its own more than the whole wage rise for café one.

Go carefully: contracted hours can only change by agreement, staff need their legal rest breaks, and an understaffed rush costs sales. The aim is to move cover to where the customers are, not to strip it out.

Quiet hours are paid for already
Fill them, don’t just cut them

If someone has to be on shift at 2:30pm anyway, every extra customer in that slot is almost pure contribution. A mid-afternoon offer aimed at people who already know you — a pastry pairing, a bonus stamp, a “bring a colleague” deal — uses staff time you are paying for regardless.

A bigger basket from the same visit
One question at the till

Selling more to customers who are already at the counter costs no extra labour. Pick one pairing and ask it the same way every time: “Anything to eat with that?” works better when there is one clear answer on the counter. Track it for a fortnight so you know whether it is working, rather than guessing.

Turning it into visits

How a loyalty programme pays part of the gap

The wage rise is an annual number. Your café runs on visits. Converting one into the other shows how reachable it is.

The gap, in cups
About 23 a week

£57.94 a week divided by £2.53 of contribution per flat white is about 23 extra cups a week for café one, and about 21 for café two. That is 23 regulars each coming in one extra time a week — or 46 coming in one extra time a fortnight.

Why regulars, not new customers
You already know them

Winning a stranger costs marketing money and time. Nudging someone who already likes your coffee to come in on a Tuesday afternoon costs a reward you control. A stamp card gives them a reason to choose you over the chain next door, and a reward only costs you when it has already brought the visits in.

One page

A three-month plan

Start with what saves money for certain, then what makes money if it works.

Month 1: check the payroll
An afternoon

Rates: confirm everyone is on at least the right rate for their age since 1 April, and diary each young employee’s next birthday. Employment Allowance: it has to be claimed every tax year — check your payroll software or HMRC account shows it for 2026/27. National Insurance categories: anyone under 21 should be on category M. Your number: redo the tables above with your own staff and hours.

Month 2: rota and basket
Four weeks of till data

Lay hourly sales next to the rota and find the two weakest staffed hours. Talk to the team before changing anything. Pick one add-on pairing and one way of asking, and count how often it lands. Decide on prices last, once you know how much of the gap the other two have closed.

Month 3: bring regulars back more often
Target: your weekly cup gap

Divide your yearly increase by 52, then by what you keep on an average sale. That is your weekly target. Put a stamp card on the counter, offer it to every customer for a week, and use your quiet hours for the first offer. At the end of the month, compare repeat visits with your target and adjust the reward or the timing.

Questions

Frequently asked

What is the National Living Wage in April 2026?

From 1 April 2026 the National Living Wage for workers aged 21 and over is £12.71 an hour, up 50p (4.1%) from £12.21. The rate for 18 to 20 year olds is £10.85, up 85p (8.5%) from £10.00. Workers under 18 and apprentices in their first year or under 19 get £8.00, up from £7.55. The rates apply across the UK.

How much extra will this cost a small café?

For a café with three full-time staff aged 22 or over on the legal minimum, 37.5 hours a week, the rise is £2,925 a year in wages plus £87.75 in pension contributions: about £3,013. Employer National Insurance adds another £438.75, but most small cafés pay none of it because the £10,500 Employment Allowance covers their whole National Insurance bill. A café with a mix of full-time, part-time and under-21 staff will typically see less, around £2,700 to £3,000, because employers pay no National Insurance on under-21s earning under £50,270 and under-22s are not automatically enrolled into a pension.

Do I pay employer National Insurance on staff under 21?

Not on earnings up to £50,270 a year. Employees under 21 go on National Insurance category M, where the employer rate is 0% up to the upper secondary threshold. Your employee still pays their own National Insurance. Payroll software applies the category from the date of birth, so check every young employee’s date of birth is correct.

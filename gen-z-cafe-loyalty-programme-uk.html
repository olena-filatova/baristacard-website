Why Gen Z Customers Ignore Your Stamp Card (and What Actually Gets Them to Join)

If your loyalty sign-ups skew older, the usual explanation is that young people are not loyal. The 2026 survey data says the opposite: Gen Z is the age group most keen to join a loyalty scheme. They are turning down the card, not the idea. This piece sets out what the numbers actually show, corrects two things most advice on this subject gets wrong, and gives you three changes that cost almost nothing.

Last updated 22 September 2026 · UK · Sources: Antavo Global Customer Loyalty Report 2026, Attentive State of Loyalty & Retention 2026

?

Why don’t Gen Z customers join my café’s loyalty programme? It is very unlikely to be a lack of interest. 60% of Gen Z say they are more inclined to join loyalty programmes than a year ago, against a 43% global average — the keenest of any age group. What they turn down is the format. Only 26.5% want a plastic card, against 41% of consumers overall, and 24% name a lengthy sign-up as a disappointment, against 16% globally. If your scheme is a physical card handed over at the till, you are asking the most enthusiastic people in your queue to do the one thing they like least.

Two corrections

What most advice about Gen Z gets wrong

Before the fixes, two claims you will read everywhere that the data does not support. Both matter, because acting on either one would send you in the wrong direction.

Not supported

“Gen Z hates apps — they’ll abandon a download”

This is the single most repeated claim in writing about younger customers and loyalty, and the survey data does not back it. Asked how they want to access a loyalty programme, 43.5% of Gen Z chose logging into a mobile app — against a global figure of 44%. That is no meaningful difference at all. On digital loyalty cards held on the phone, it is 42.5% for Gen Z against 42% globally. Also identical.

So “get rid of the app” is not the lesson. “Get rid of the plastic” is — that is where the 14.5-point gap actually sits. Younger customers are not allergic to software. They are unwilling to carry a card, and quick to give up on a sign-up that stalls.

The practical difference matters. If you believed the myth, you might conclude your scheme needs a whole different technology. It does not. It needs the card to live in the phone the customer already has out, and the sign-up to take one action instead of five.

Overstated

“Discounts don’t move Gen Z — only status does”

Half true, and the half that is wrong will cost you money if you act on it. Money-saving is still the biggest single reason Gen Z joins a scheme, at 51%. It is just far less dominant than for older customers, and the gradient by age is remarkably clean.

They are also slightly more promotion-driven than average in their day-to-day shopping: 72% of Gen Z say promotions influence their purchases, against 69% globally. Anyone telling you this group does not respond to a good offer is not reading the same data.

The correct conclusion is additive, not substitutive: keep the free coffee, then add something money cannot buy. Stripping the discount out and replacing it with a badge would remove the reason half of them joined.

If your regulars are mostly over 50, a discount-led scheme is close to optimal. The younger your counter skews, the more you are leaving on the table by offering nothing else.

What “early access” means in a café
Costs you nothing

You do not run seasonal sales, so translate it. Early access in a café is: the pumpkin latte is on for members on Thursday and everyone else on Friday. The new single-origin is behind the counter for card holders before it goes on the board. The Christmas blend is held back — two kilos, members only, until it sells out.

None of that is a discount. You are selling the same coffee at the same price. The only thing you have given away is order, and order is free.

A second, separate benefit: it gives you a genuine reason to message your members that is not “come back, here’s 10% off”. Which channel actually brings regulars back covers how to send that without annoying anyone.

Recognition is the cheapest version of VIP
And the hardest to copy

“VIP treatment” in a survey sounds like airport lounges. In an independent café it means someone starting your drink before you have asked for it. That is a thing a chain genuinely cannot do at eleven thousand sites, and it is the one advantage you have that no amount of loyalty software buys.

The practical version: if your scheme shows you a name and an order history at the till, use it out loud. “Oat flat white, Maya?” does more for a nineteen-year-old’s attachment to your café than a tenth stamp.

Worth noting where the limit is: Gen Z reported feeling valued by loyalty programmes at 57%, against 56% globally — no different. Nobody is getting this right yet, chains included. That is the opening.

Where you actually lose them

The sign-up, in the thirty seconds you have

Two of the strongest Gen Z signals in the whole survey are about friction, not reward: lengthy sign-ups disappoint 24% of them against 16% globally, and technical errors 28.5% against 24%.

The queue is the whole constraint
Not the offer

A customer will give you the time between paying and their drink arriving. In a morning rush, that is under a minute, and they are aware of the person behind them. Anything that needs an email address typed on a phone with one hand loses to simply not bothering — and the younger the customer, the more likely they are to abandon it.

So the test for your sign-up is not “is it easy?” It is: can this be finished, start to end, before the drink lands? If it cannot, your sign-up rate is capped by something that has nothing to do with how good your reward is. Why loyalty sign-ups stall in the morning rush works through that in more detail.

Why the plastic card fails twice
At sign-up and after

The physical card looks like the low-friction option, because handing one over takes two seconds. It fails later. To use it, the customer has to have it on them — in a wallet, in a bag, in a coat they are not wearing today. For a customer who carries a phone and a bank card and nothing else, that is a card that will be at home every time.

A card saved into the phone’s wallet does not have that problem, and it is the one format where Gen Z matches everyone else at 42.5%. It is also the format that needs nothing from your staff: the customer scans, it saves, done.

What to do

Three low-cost changes for a younger counter

In this order, because the first one closes the biggest measured gap and none of the three needs a bigger reward.

Change one

Move the card into the phone

This is the 14.5-point gap, and it is the only change on this page that addresses it. A pass that saves into Apple or Google Wallet needs no app download, and it cannot be left at home.

Cost: none if your provider offers it. Watch for: keep honouring paper cards for a few weeks so your older regulars are not pushed out while you do this.

Change two

Make it a scan with nothing to type

A code on the counter the customer can scan themselves, with no email or phone number required to start. Add the details later, if at all — every field you ask for at the till is a reason to stop.

Cost: a printed sign. Watch for: where it sits. It needs to be readable while they are waiting, not on the wall behind you.

Change three

Give members first refusal on one thing

One item, one day early, or one small batch held back. Not a discount — access. Tell them it is because they are on the card, and the mechanism becomes the point.

Cost: nothing but sequence. Watch for: it must be real. A “members-only” offer that everybody gets anyway is worse than not running one.

A word on tiers, points and badges. If you are running a stamp card in one café, resist them. Gen Z responds to exclusivity, which is not the same as responding to complexity, and there is no evidence in this data that a tier system does anything for a nine-stamp coffee card. Tiers make sense when you have enough members for the top tier to feel scarce. Until then, the same instinct is better served by a member-only bean and knowing people’s names. Questions

Frequently asked

Why don’t Gen Z customers join my café’s loyalty programme?

It is very unlikely to be a lack of interest. In Antavo’s Global Customer Loyalty Report 2026, which surveyed 10,000 consumers and 3,000 marketers, 60% of Gen Z said they were more inclined to join loyalty programmes than the year before, against a global average of 43% — the keenest of any age group. What they turn down is the format. Only 26.5% of Gen Z said they wanted a plastic card, against 41% of consumers overall, and 24% named a lengthy sign-up as a source of disappointment, against 16% globally. So if your scheme is a physical card handed over at the till, or a sign-up that needs a form, you are asking the most enthusiastic group in the queue to do the one thing they like least.

Do Gen Z customers use loyalty programmes?

Yes, and more readily than older customers. Antavo’s 2026 report found 60% of Gen Z more inclined to join than the previous year, compared with a 43% global average, and 72% said promotions influence their purchases, slightly above the 69% global figure. The gap is not in willingness, it is in what they will accept: plastic cards drop to 26.5% for Gen Z against 41% overall, while a digital loyalty card on the phone sits at 42.5%, essentially the same as everyone else.

Does Gen Z dislike loyalty apps?

Not according to the data, and this is the most common mistake in advice written about this group. In Antavo’s 2026 survey, 43.5% of Gen Z chose logging into a mobile app as a way of accessing a loyalty programme, against a global figure of 44% — statistically no different. Gen Z is not anti-app. The real difference is that they are markedly less willing to carry a plastic card, and more easily put off by a slow or broken sign-up. Widely quoted figures claiming that most loyalty apps are deleted within days are a separate matter: we could not trace any of them to a named study, so we do not repeat them.

What do Gen Z customers want from a loyalty scheme instead of discounts?

Saving money is still their single biggest reason to join, so do not remove it. It is simply less dominant than for older customers: 51% of Gen Z named money-saving benefits as a motivation, against 62.3% of Millennials, 72.3% of Gen X and 80.5% of Baby Boomers. Where Gen Z pulls ahead is exclusivity and access — early access to sales at 30% against a 26% global figure, brand collaborations at 24% against 16%, and VIP treatment at 22.5% against 15%. In a café that translates into first refusal on a new seasonal drink or a limited bean, a members-only hour, or being recognised by name. Those additions cost little; the discount is what still gets most of them through the door.

How can a small café make its loyalty scheme work for younger customers?

Three changes, in this order. First, replace the physical card with one that saves into the phone’s wallet, because the card format is the largest measured gap between Gen Z and everyone else. Second, make the sign-up a scan the customer does themselves, with nothing to type, since a lengthy sign-up disappoints Gen Z at one and a half times the global rate. Third, add one piece of access rather than a bigger discount: tell members about a new drink a day early, or hold back a small batch for them. None of the three requires a bigger reward or more staff time.

Sources, and what we could not verify

The sample is global, not UK-only, and Antavo sells loyalty software, so we have used the figures for the shape of the differences between age groups rather than as precise UK numbers.

Separately, Attentive’s 2026 State of Loyalty & Retention report found 39% of Gen Z wanted early access to product launches, against 32% of Millennials — the same direction as Antavo’s finding. We have not led with it because the sample is 600 US shoppers, which is small and not a UK picture.

Checked and not used. Two sources that circulate widely on this topic did not survive a check. One frequently cited guide gives a set of confident Gen Z loyalty figures — 72% belonging to at least one programme, 37% going inactive within six months, a 61% push-notification opt-in rate — with no named study, sample or date attached to any of them, so none appear here. Nor do the wallet-versus-app adoption statistics you will find repeated across the industry: every version we traced (“80% of customers dislike downloading new apps”, “83% uninstalled within 30 days”, “90% abandonment in the first week”) led back to a supplier’s own blog with nothing behind it. We have also not quoted a “Gen Z spending power” figure for the same reason. The argument on this page does not need any of them, and a number you cannot check is worse than no number.

Double-Stamp Days vs Discount Codes: Which Bonfire Night Promo Actually Brings Regulars Back

Both cost you almost exactly the same per cup. The difference is when you pay, and who you pay it to. One is a certain cost today, handed to everyone including the people who were coming anyway. The other costs nothing until somebody comes back enough times to earn it.

Last updated 23 September 2026 · Worked in 2026 UK prices · VAT treatment per HMRC Notice 709/1

?

Should my café run a discount or a loyalty promotion for Bonfire Night? On a £3.40 flat white the two cost you the same: a 20% discount code costs 56.7p of net revenue, and a double-stamp day creates 56.7p of reward liability. But the discount is certain, immediate, and goes to everyone who asks — including regulars who would have paid full price. The double stamp is only ever paid if that customer comes back enough times to finish the card. If your aim is a busy week, discount. If your aim is regulars in December, double stamps.

Start here

Both promos cost the same. That is not the interesting part

Before you can compare two mechanics you need one number: what a stamp is worth. Most owners have never worked it out, and guess far too low.

Flat white, takeaway, menu price £3.40
VAT at 20% — hot drinks to take away are standard-rated −57p
What actually reaches your till £2.83
So a free drink on a 10-stamp card costs £2.83
Which makes one stamp worth 28.3p

The free cup does not cost you the beans and the paper. It costs you the sale you did not ring up — the full net menu price. We work through that in detail in loyalty reward maths, including why the stamp count matters less than owners think.

Option A

A 20% discount code

The customer pays £2.72 instead of £3.40. After VAT you keep £2.27 instead of £2.83.

You lose 56.7p per cup — now, and for certain.

Option B

A double-stamp day

The customer pays the full £3.40 and takes two stamps instead of one. Two stamps is 2 × 28.3p of future reward.

You owe 56.7p per cup — later, and only if the card finishes.

The numbers are identical. That is worth sitting with, because it means the choice between these two promotions is not a question of which is cheaper. It is a question of what you are buying with the same 56.7p.

The real difference

A discount is paid on the visit. A stamp is paid on the return

  20% discount code Double-stamp day
Cost per cup 56.7p 56.7p
When you pay it At the till, that day Only when a card completes
Paid even if they never return Yes No
Goes to people already buying Yes, in full Only as progress
Reaches strangers walking past Yes Only if they join
What the customer ends up holding A cheaper coffee A card closer to finished
Effect on your normal price Resets it downwards Leaves it alone

Worked on a £3.40 takeaway flat white and a 10-stamp card. Change the price or the stamp count and both columns move together — the comparison does not.

The line worth reading twice is the last one. A discount code teaches the customer what your coffee is really worth to you, and some of them will wait for the next one. A stamp never touches your menu price; it changes how close somebody is to something they already wanted.

Why it works on some people and not others

Proximity to the reward matters more than the size of it

A double-stamp day does not pull equally on everybody holding a card. It pulls hardest on the people who were already close.

At 1–2 stamps Barely moves. The reward is too far off to feel real.
At 4–5 stamps Some pull. Halfway is where a card starts to matter.
At 7–8 stamps Strongest pull. Two stamps finishes the card this week.

This is the goal-gradient effect, and the study behind it was run in a café. Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng tracked customers on a buy-ten-get-one-free coffee card and found people bought more frequently as they got closer to the free cup. The same paper found that an illusion of progress does the same job: a twelve-stamp card with two stamps already on it beats a ten-stamp card, even though both need ten purchases. Joseph Nunes and Xavier Drèze showed the same thing at a car wash — 300 customers, eight washes required either way, and the cards that arrived with two stamps already applied were redeemed by 34% of customers against 19% for the plain ones.

Worth being straight about this: neither of those studies tested a double-stamp day. They tested head starts and the effect of being near a goal. Applying them to a double-stamp week is a reasonable inference — it moves the same lever, distance from the reward — but it is an inference, not a measured result. We could not find any published study measuring a seasonal double-stamp promotion in a UK café, so nobody can honestly give you a percentage for this. What we can give you is the mechanics and your own till data.
After the fireworks

Which one actually produces a visit in December

The discount code path

Somebody comes in on 5 November with a code, pays £2.72, and leaves. You are 56.7p down. Whether they return depends entirely on whether they liked the coffee — the promotion itself has left nothing behind. If they were a regular already, you have simply given a regular 68p.

The double-stamp path

Somebody comes in on 5 November, pays full price, and leaves holding a card that is two steps further on. You are 56.7p committed but nothing has left the till. The next visit is now worth more to them than it was last week — and that next visit is the thing you were actually trying to buy.

The honest catch, from the same research. Kivetz and colleagues also found a post-reward reset: once a customer redeems, their purchase rate drops back before building again. A double-stamp week pulls redemptions forward, so expect a cluster of free cups in mid-November and a quieter fortnight behind it. That is not the promotion failing. It is the promotion working early — but if you budget for the free cups in December and they all land in November, it will feel like failing.
Putting it to work

A one-week Bonfire Night promo using the scheme you already have

No new codes, no reprinting, no second system to explain at the till.

Step 1

Give it a hard end date

“Double stamps until 5 November” beats “double stamps this autumn”. An offer with no end is not an offer, it is your new prices.

The date does the work: a deadline people can picture is what turns intention into a visit this week.

Step 2

Split the promo by who you are talking to

Existing members: double stamps. It costs nothing today and pulls hardest on the ones closest to a reward.

New sign-ups that week: start their card with two stamps already on it. That is the head start the car-wash study measured, and it is cheaper than a discount.

Step 3

Check one number afterwards

Not takings that week — takings always rise during a promotion. Count how many cards completed in November against a normal month, then watch whether those people came back in December.

That is the only number that separates a loyalty promotion from a discount wearing a costume.

Questions

Frequently asked

What is a double-stamp day for a café loyalty card?

It is a limited-time promotion where every qualifying purchase earns two stamps instead of one, so a customer reaches their free drink in half the visits. On a ten-stamp card, somebody sitting on seven stamps can finish it in two visits rather than three. The point is not the extra generosity; it is that it pulls hardest on the customers who were already close to a reward, which is exactly the group most likely to come back anyway if you give them a reason this week rather than next month.

Do loyalty promotions work better than discounts for seasonal footfall?

They do a different job, and which is better depends on what you are short of. A discount code reaches anyone, including people who have never been in, and buys you a busy day — but you pay for it at the till, immediately, including on every regular who would have paid full price. A double-stamp day only reaches people already in your scheme, and only costs you anything if they come back enough times to finish the card. If you need bodies through the door this Saturday, discount. If what you actually want is more regulars in December, the stamp promotion is the one that leaves something behind.

How much does a double-stamp day actually cost me?

Work it from the reward, not the ingredients. A free £3.40 takeaway flat white costs you £2.83, because hot drinks to take away are standard-rated for VAT and the 57p is not yours. Divide that by the number of stamps on your card — on a ten-stamp card each stamp is worth 28.3p, so a double-stamp day adds 28.3p of liability per cup sold. That is the same 56.7p a 20% discount code would cost you outright, with one difference: the liability only ever becomes a real cost if that customer completes the card. Cards that are never finished cost you nothing at all.

Will a double-stamp day just bring forward free coffees I was going to give away anyway?

Partly, yes, and you should plan for it. Research on loyalty cards has consistently found that customers accelerate as they approach a reward and then slow down after redeeming it. So a promotion that pushes people over the line produces a cluster of free cups sooner than you would otherwise have seen, followed by a quieter spell. What you are buying is the extra visits that happened because of the deadline — the ones that would have drifted into December or not happened at all. Whether you got any is measurable: compare completed cards in November against a normal month, and then check December.

Should I run a Halloween promo and a Bonfire Night promo, or one?

One, with a single end date. The two events are six days apart, and running back-to-back offers through late October and early November teaches regulars that there is always something on, which is the fastest way to stop anyone paying full price. A single week ending on 5 November gives you one deadline to talk about, one thing for staff to explain, and a clean before-and-after to measure. If you want the Halloween week to do something as well, make it a sign-up push rather than a second discount.

Where the facts come from

Sources, and what we could not verify

The maths. All figures are worked from a £3.40 takeaway flat white at 2026 UK prices. Hot drinks sold to take away are standard-rated at 20% VAT under HMRC VAT Notice 709/1, giving 56.7p of VAT and £2.83 net. A 20% discount code reduces the price to £2.72, of which 45.3p is VAT, leaving £2.27 net — a loss of 56.7p. On a ten-stamp card, one stamp carries £2.83 ÷ 10 = 28.3p of future reward, so a double stamp is 56.7p. The two match exactly; that is arithmetic, not a coincidence, because a 20% discount and a doubled stamp on a ten-stamp card are both one tenth of the net price twice over.

The psychology. The goal-gradient finding is Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng, “The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected”, Journal of Marketing Research 43(1), 2006, which tracked real café customers on a buy-ten-get-one-free card and found purchase rates accelerated closer to the reward, along with the post-reward reset. The head-start finding is Nunes and Drèze, “The Endowed Progress Effect”, Journal of Consumer Research 32(4), 2006: 300 car-wash customers, eight purchases required in both conditions, 34% redemption on the pre-stamped card against 19% on the plain one.

Checked and not used. We could not find any published study measuring a double-stamp promotion, a Bonfire Night promotion, or any seasonal loyalty mechanic in a UK café. Both studies above are US, both are twenty years old, and neither tested doubling the rate at which stamps are earned — they tested distance from the reward, which is the same lever pulled a different way. We have therefore written this page as mechanics and arithmetic rather than dressing it in borrowed percentages. If you see a confident figure for how much a double-stamp week lifts repeat visits, ask what it is measuring: we could not trace one to a study.

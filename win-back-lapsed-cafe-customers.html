Why your regulars stop coming in (and how to get them back)

You can name six people you have not seen in a month. The tall americano before the school run. The two who always took the window table on a Thursday. You do not know whether they moved job, moved house or moved café — and you have no way to ask. Here is what is actually happening, and the sequence that gets some of them back.

The short version: for a café the window to win someone back is roughly three to four weeks, not the ninety days e-commerce advice assumes — because a coffee habit is daily, and a replacement habit forms in about two months. Send three messages: recognition at day 21, a free add-on at day 30, one last note at day 45. Then stop.

The short answer: the window is weeks, not months

Almost everything written about winning back lapsed customers was written for online shops. It assumes a sixty to ninety day window, a discount code, and a mental model borrowed from the abandoned basket. None of that transfers to a café, and following it will make you late.

The reason is arithmetic rather than opinion. An online shop might reasonably expect a customer every few months, so ninety days of silence is a soft signal. Your customers came in three, five, seven times a week. Three weeks of silence from someone who used to come every morning is not a soft signal — it is roughly fifteen missed visits, and it means something changed.

The café-specific guidance published by Regulr, a reactivation platform, puts the window at twenty-one to twenty-eight days, and says the habit is effectively broken by around six weeks. Treat that as a vendor's rule of thumb rather than a finding, because that is what it is. But the underlying behavioural science supports the shape of it, and that part is not a vendor's opinion.

Why regulars actually stop coming

The habit broke, not the relationship

This is the one that matters most, and it is the one owners are least likely to believe, because it feels personal when someone stops coming.

Buying a coffee from you was almost certainly not a decision your customer made each morning. It was a habit — a behaviour welded to a context, running with very little conscious thought. The most-cited study of how habits form, from University College London, followed ninety-six people repeating a daily behaviour and found the median time to reach near-automatic performance was sixty-six days, with a range from eighteen to two hundred and fifty-four.

That number cuts two ways, and both are useful to you.

The reassuring half: the same study found that missing a single day did not measurably damage the habit. One skipped morning is noise. Two are noise. Your customer does not need rescuing because they were off last Tuesday.

The urgent half: if it takes something like two months to build a coffee habit, then a customer who has been going somewhere else for two months has finished building a new one — and you are now asking them to break a habit rather than resume one. That is a completely different and much harder request. It is why waiting ninety days, the way the e-commerce playbooks tell you to, means arriving after the job has been done by somebody else.

The practical version

You are not trying to rekindle a relationship. You are trying to interrupt a habit before it hardens — theirs and, if you are honest, yours. Three weeks is early enough that the old pattern is still the path of least resistance. Ten weeks is not.

A route change, not a rejection

The second most common reason is geography, and it has nothing to do with you. A new job. A different school. A team that went to three days in the office instead of five. A bus route rerouted for roadworks. Your café was on a path, and the path moved.

This matters because it changes what you should say. A customer whose commute changed did not decide your flat white was worse. Sending them an apologetic message, or a discount that implies you are trying to buy back their approval, misreads the situation. Sometimes the honest answer is that they cannot come at eight in the morning any more, but they could come at eleven on a Saturday — and the only way you find that out is by opening a door rather than making an offer.

The small things: one bad coffee, one long queue, one closed Monday

The third category is the one nobody tells you about, because people do not complain about it. They just stop.

A single sour flat white on a bad grinder day. A queue that was six deep when they had eight minutes. A Monday you closed for a deep clean, with a sign on the door they did not see until they had walked over. None of these is a scandal, and none of them is worth a complaint, so you never hear about it. What you get instead is a person who quietly stops being a regular.

This category is also the one a win-back message most reliably fixes. The barrier is small and slightly embarrassing on both sides, and being noticed is often enough to dissolve it.

How to spot a lapsed customer when you have no data

Here is the honest problem with everything above: you cannot act on any of it if you do not know who has stopped coming.

Most independent cafés genuinely do not know. Cash and contactless tell you what was sold, not who bought it. A paper stamp card lives in the customer's wallet, so a card that stops being presented is invisible to you — the only place its absence is recorded is the pocket of the person who left. Your best instrument is memory, and memory is systematically unreliable in the direction that hurts: you notice the friendly ones, the ones who chatted, the ones with dogs. The quiet Tuesday regular who never said much and spent four pounds a day for two years leaves without a trace.

There are three routes out of this, and it is worth being straight about the trade-offs.

  1. The notebook. Write down the names you can remember, with roughly when you last saw them. It costs nothing, works today, and captures perhaps the top twenty of your regulars — the memorable ones. It will miss the quiet middle, which is where most of your revenue actually sits.
  2. The till report. If you take enough card payments, some systems will show returning card tokens. It is partial, awkward to read, and it identifies a card rather than a person — but it is real data and you may already be paying for it.
  3. A digital stamp card. The reason this exists is precisely the problem above: if the card is on the customer's phone and the stamps are on your side of the glass, then "who has not been in for twenty-one days" stops being a feat of memory and becomes a list. That is the honest case for it. It is not that a digital card makes coffee better; it is that it is the only one of these three that sees the quiet Tuesday regular leave.

Whichever you choose, choose one. A win-back sequence you cannot address is a strategy that never gets sent.

The three-message win-back sequence

Copy these, change the names, and keep them short. Every one of them should be readable on a lock screen without opening anything.

Message 1 — around day 21: recognition, no offer

The first message must not sell anything. That is not politeness, it is strategy: an offer arriving out of nowhere reads as marketing and gets deleted with the rest of the marketing. An observation reads as a person noticing, which is a different category of thing entirely.

Message 1 — copy it, change the name

Subject: Haven't seen you in a bit

Hi Sam — we noticed you haven't been in for a few weeks and just wanted to say we hope everything's alright. Your stamps are still here whenever you're passing. — Everyone at Bramble & Bean

No offer, no discount code, no "we miss you!!!". If they reply to tell you they have moved to Leeds, that is a useful outcome too — it takes them off the list honestly.

Message 2 — around day 30: the add-on, not the discount

If nothing came back from the first message, now you give them a reason. Make it something you hand over, not something you take off the price.

Message 2 — copy it, change the name

Subject: A pastry on us, next time you're in

Hi Sam — next time you're passing, order whatever you like and we'll add a pastry on the house. No code, no catch, just tell whoever's on the till. You've got 4 stamps waiting too — 2 more and your next one's free.

Notice what the last line does. It is not an offer; it is a reminder of something they already own. More on why that matters below.

Message 3 — around day 45: the last one, and then stop

The third message should say, in as many words, that it is the last one. This is not a threat and it should not be dressed as scarcity. It is a courtesy, and it performs better than pressure because it removes the suspicion that you are going to keep going.

Message 3 — copy it, change the name

Subject: Last one from us

Hi Sam — we won't keep emailing. Your 4 stamps stay on your card, and the pastry offer stands whenever you're next passing. If you'd rather we stopped altogether, unsubscribe below and we'll leave you be. Thanks for the coffees. — Bramble & Bean

Then actually stop. Silence is the fourth message, and it is the one that keeps you welcome.

Why the expiring-stamp message beats the discount message

Of everything on this page, this is the single highest-leverage idea, and it costs nothing to apply.

People weigh losing something they already have far more heavily than gaining something equivalent. This is the best-established finding in behavioural economics, and it means a message about four stamps the customer has already earned and could lose does more work than an offer of something new worth the same money. The stamps are theirs. They did the buying. You are not giving them anything — you are reminding them of a thing they own that is sitting idle.

A percentage discount does the opposite of this, in three ways at once.

  • It reframes your coffee as something with a negotiable price, and teaches your best customers that waiting produces a better one.
  • It comes off a margin you cannot afford, at retail value, on a product you already priced carefully.
  • It reads as a transaction — you want something and you are paying for it — where a free pastry reads as a gift, and gifts create a sense of obligation that discounts do not.

If you take one thing from this page: lead with what they already have, not with what you will knock off.

When to stop and let a customer go

Three messages, then nothing. This is a rule and it is worth holding to when the temptation is to send a fourth.

People push back against attempts to control their choices, and the harder the push the stronger the resistance. A fourth message does not read as persistence; it reads as pressure, and pressure gets you unsubscribed. That is the outcome to be genuinely afraid of, because the asymmetry is brutal: a customer who has drifted away can still wander back in on a Saturday for no reason at all, but a customer who has opted out has closed the door, and you no longer have a way to tell them when you get the espresso machine you have been saving for.

A quiet gap is recoverable. An unsubscribe is not. Never trade the first for the second in exchange for one more send.

And keep some perspective about how much of this is yours to fix. Foodservice customer traffic across the UK fell 2.3% in the first quarter of 2026, the steepest drop since January 2024, according to figures reported by UKHospitality, with casual dining and pub visits down considerably more. Some of your missing regulars are not choosing a competitor. They are making fewer coffees a week full stop, and no sequence of messages changes that. Winning back the ones you can is worth doing precisely because the tide is going the other way.

What to measure, so you know it worked

Keep this small enough that you will actually do it between the morning rush and the lunch one.

  • How many people you contacted, and on what day of their gap. Without this the rest means nothing.
  • How many came back within fourteen days of a message. This is the number that matters, and it is the only one that is really about the campaign.
  • Whether they stayed. Check again at sixty days. A returner who came once for a free pastry and never again is a cost, not a win, and you want to know which of the three messages produces people who stick.
  • How many unsubscribed. If this climbs above a small handful, your messages are too frequent or too salesy — usually the second.
  • What it cost you. Add up the add-ons you gave away at wholesale, not at menu price. It is normally a much smaller number than owners fear, which is the point.

Run the whole thing once, on a list of twenty people, before you build anything more elaborate. One honest cycle will teach you more about your own customers than any article can, including this one.

Before you send anything

A win-back message is marketing, and marketing to individuals by email or text is governed by rules about consent. Getting that right is not optional and it is not complicated once you have seen it laid out — we have written the café-shaped version in café loyalty schemes and UK GDPR. Read it before your first send, not after.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

How do I get lapsed customers to come back to my coffee shop?

Reach out about three weeks after their last visit, while the habit is still fresh. Send three messages and no more: at around day 21 a short note that simply says you have noticed they have not been in, with no offer attached; at around day 30 a free add-on rather than a percentage discount; at around day 45 a final one, and then stop. Recognition first, offer second, silence third.

How long before a customer counts as lapsed for a café?

Far sooner than the ninety days that e-commerce advice assumes. A coffee habit runs daily or twice weekly, so three to four weeks without a visit is already a meaningful gap — the reactivation guidance published by Regulr puts the café window at 21 to 28 days. Judge it against that customer's own rhythm: three weeks is nothing for someone who came monthly and a long time for someone who came every morning.

Should I offer a discount to win back a customer?

Not as your first move, and preferably not at all. A percentage discount teaches regulars that waiting produces a better price, and it comes straight off a margin that is already thin. A free add-on — the pastry, the extra shot, the size upgrade — reads as a gift rather than a transaction, costs you the wholesale price rather than the retail one, and does not reset what people expect to pay.

How many win-back messages should I send?

Three, then stop. Someone who has ignored three messages is telling you something, and a fourth converts a customer who might drift back on their own into one who unsubscribes. An unsubscribe is permanent in a way that a quiet gap is not, and you lose the ability to reach them when you actually have something worth saying.

Is SMS or email better for a café win-back campaign?

Whichever you have genuine permission for. Text is more immediate and more intrusive, which cuts both ways, and the consent rules are the same for both. The bigger question is not the channel but whether you can tell who has lapsed in the first place — most independent cafés cannot, which is why the campaign never gets sent at all.

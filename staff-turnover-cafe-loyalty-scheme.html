The Hidden Cost of Staff Turnover on Your Café’s Loyalty Scheme

Every guide to growing your loyalty sign-ups quietly assumes one thing: a settled team who all explain the scheme the same way. Most independent cafés do not have that, and never will. This is what high turnover actually does to a loyalty scheme, which part of it breaks first, and how to build one that does not care who is on the till this morning.

Last updated 22 September 2026 · UK · Sources: Harri Peach Report 2026, Dojo UK Inflation Index, Pineapple & Sona, RotaCloud, CIPD/ONS

?

Does staff turnover affect a café’s loyalty programme? Almost certainly, because your scheme is explained to customers by whoever is on the till — and in UK hospitality only about one in four new hires is still there after 90 days. In restaurants and cafés, employer-reported turnover runs at around 39% a year. So on any given shift, a good share of the people pitching your scheme learned it in a rush, from someone who was also fairly new. No published study measures loyalty sign-ups against staff tenure, so treat this as an explanation to test rather than a proven number: watch whether your sign-up rate dips in the fortnight after you take someone on.

The problem

Your loyalty scheme is only as consistent as your newest hire

A loyalty scheme looks like a piece of software. In practice, most of it is a sentence somebody says out loud, forty times a morning, to strangers.

The pitch is the only optional part of the transaction
And it is invisible when it goes

Think about what a day-one hire is holding in their head during a morning rush: the order, the till, the machine, the milk, the person waiting, the person after them. Every one of those has an immediate consequence if they get it wrong. Somebody says something.

The loyalty pitch is the only part with no consequence at all if it is skipped. The queue moves faster. Nobody complains. The customer does not know they were owed a sentence. And you do not find out until you look at your sign-up numbers weeks later — by which time you have hired somebody else and the pattern has repeated.

That is why turnover hits loyalty harder than it hits coffee quality. Bad coffee gets sent back. A missing pitch just quietly does not happen.

You explain it in shorthand you have forgotten is shorthand
The curse of knowing your own scheme

You have explained your scheme a thousand times. Your longest-serving barista has explained it several hundred. Both of you have compressed it down to something like “do you want to go on the card?” — which works perfectly, because your regulars already know what the card is.

A new starter hears that sentence and copies it. To a first-time customer it means nothing, so it gets a no, and after a dozen nos the new starter reasonably concludes that customers are not interested and stops asking. Nobody did anything wrong. The scheme was handed over in a compressed form that only works on people who already understand it.

This is the part most owners get wrong when they diagnose the problem. They think the new hire needs more training. Usually the new hire needs a longer sentence.

Customers notice inconsistency before they notice the scheme
And they read it as the café, not the staff

A loyalty scheme works because it feels like an ongoing arrangement between a customer and a place. Stamped on Monday, not offered on Tuesday, offered twice on Wednesday, told something different about the reward on Thursday — and it stops feeling like an arrangement and starts feeling like a lottery.

Customers do not attribute that to the rota. They attribute it to you. “They’re a bit hit and miss with that card thing” is a sentence about the café, and once somebody has said it to themselves they stop bringing their phone out.

What that means on your rota

The maths on a six-person team

This is arithmetic from the figures above, not a measured finding. Run it with your own numbers.

  1. You have six people on the books. At the 39.1% café and restaurant rate, roughly two and a third leave in a year.
  2. But if only one in four new starters reaches 90 days, most of the people you take on do not become one of your six. To net two and a third lasting replacements you have to hire substantially more than two and a third people — and every one of them spends their first weeks at the till learning your scheme.
  3. So the honest way to think about it is not “I lose two people a year”. It is: there is almost always somebody new on the front counter. Not occasionally. As a standing condition.
  4. That flips the question. The useful question is not “how do I train everyone properly?” It is “how much of my loyalty scheme still works when the person serving has been here a week?”

Be sceptical of cost-per-hire figures. You will see large round numbers for “the cost of replacing a hospitality worker”, usually published by someone selling recruitment or rota software, and usually without a traceable method. We are not quoting one. The number you can actually stand behind is your own: the paid hours you spend on a new starter before they can work a shift unsupervised, times your hourly rate, times the number of people you took on last year. That figure is defensible, and it is the one that matters for a decision about your own café.

In order

What breaks first — the pitch, the stamp, or the reward

Three things can go wrong, and they fail in a predictable order. Each one has a different tell, so you can work out which is happening in your café.

1. The sign-up pitch, almost always

This goes first because it is the only part nobody is waiting for. It is also the part that needs the most confidence: you are asking a stranger for something in front of a queue. A new starter who is not yet sure of the answer to “what do I get?” will avoid opening that conversation, and after a week of avoiding it the habit is set.

What makes it worse is that the pitch is the only part of your scheme with no record of not happening. A missed stamp leaves an annoyed customer. A missed pitch leaves nothing.

The tell: new sign-ups fall but your existing members’ visit frequency holds steady.

2. Stamp accuracy, in both directions

New staff err in whichever direction feels safer to them, and it is rarely consistent. Some forget entirely, because scanning is one more thing in an unfamiliar sequence. Some over-award — two stamps for two coffees on one order when your scheme says one per visit, or a stamp for a bottle of water — because being generous feels less risky than arguing with a customer.

Over-awarding is the quieter and more expensive failure. It does not generate a complaint, so you only see it in your margin, and it changes what your regulars expect. Once a customer has been given two stamps in a visit, the person who gives them one next week looks mean.

The tell: rewards are being redeemed sooner than your stamp count should allow, or stamps-per-visit is drifting above one.

3. Reward redemption, the worst one to get wrong

This is the rarest failure and by far the most damaging, because it happens at the moment a customer has finally earned something. A new starter who does not know how to redeem, or who is not sure the customer is entitled, has three options in front of a queue: guess, refuse, or fetch someone. All three are bad, and refusing is the one that loses the customer.

A regular who is told “I don’t think that’s right” when they present nine stamps does not come back and try again next week. They have just been told, in public, that their loyalty was not real.

The tell: you cannot spot this one in the numbers. You have to ask. “Has anyone had trouble claiming a free coffee lately?” at the end of a shift will find it.

Why this order, not a measured one. This sequence is reasoned from how the three tasks differ — whether skipping them has an immediate consequence, and how much confidence each needs — not from published data comparing them. We could not find any. It is offered as a diagnostic to check against your own numbers, and the tells above are there so you can.

Day one

The five-minute version any new starter can use

Not a training programme. One sentence, one rule, and one thing to point at.

The sentence

“Your tenth coffee’s on us — want me to set that up? It’s one tap, no app.”

Three things are doing the work there, and it is worth knowing why so you can adapt it to your own reward.

  1. What they get comes first, and it is concrete. Not “our loyalty scheme”. A free coffee. Lead with the thing, never the mechanism.
  2. The effort is named and it is small. “One tap, no app” answers the objection the customer has not said out loud yet, which is that this will take a minute and clutter their phone.
  3. It ends in a closed question. “Want me to set that up?” can be answered with a nod while they get their card out. “Would you like to hear about our loyalty scheme?” requires them to agree to a conversation, which in a queue almost nobody does.

The rule for anything they cannot answer

Tell them, on day one, in these words: if a customer asks something about the card you do not know, give them the benefit of the doubt if it is small, and take their name and my number if it is not. Small means a stamp or a single free coffee. That is worth a few pounds. An unhappy regular is worth considerably more, and a new starter who is scared of getting it wrong will avoid the whole subject rather than risk it.

The thing to point at

A sign on the counter the customer can scan themselves. It means a new starter who freezes mid-sentence has somewhere to send the customer, and it means the sign-up can happen even when the pitch does not. Sign-up friction in the morning rush goes into where that sign needs to sit and why the position matters more than the wording.

What not to cover on day one

Tiers, bonus days, referral rewards, the dashboard, the rules for multi-item orders. All of it can wait. A new starter who knows one sentence perfectly will sign up more customers than one who half-remembers seven rules, and every extra rule you add on day one is another reason to say nothing at all.

The structural fix

Take your staff out of the sign-up entirely

Training fights turnover one hire at a time and loses. The alternative is to reduce how much of the scheme depends on a person remembering anything.

Step one

Let the customer sign themselves up

A scannable sign on the counter turns the sign-up from something your staff must perform into something a customer can do while they wait for their flat white. The pitch becomes a nudge towards the sign rather than a full explanation, which is a much easier thing to ask of somebody in their first week.

Step two

Put the card in the customer’s phone, not in your drawer

A paper card behind the counter needs finding, matching to a name and stamping — three chances for a new starter to slow the queue or get it wrong. A pass saved in the customer’s own wallet needs none of that, and it cannot be lost by your team, only by them.

Step three

Make the reward apply itself

If the system shows the reward and marks it used, redemption stops depending on whether the person serving knows the rule. That removes the failure that costs you the most, and it is the one part of this you should not compromise on.

One honest caveat about the numbers you will see quoted here. There are widely repeated statistics comparing wallet-pass and app adoption rates — figures like “90% of loyalty apps are deleted within a week”. We have deliberately not used them. Every version we could trace led back to a vendor blog with no named study, method or sample behind it. The argument above does not need them: a digital pass genuinely requires fewer steps from your staff than a paper card, and you can verify that in your own café in a morning. We would rather give you a claim you can check than a percentage you cannot.

Diagnose before you change anything

How to check whether this is actually your problem

Four checks, none of which need new software. Do them before you rewrite your scheme.

  1. Line your sign-ups up against your start dates. Write down the week each of your last few hires started. Then look at new loyalty sign-ups per week. If sign-ups dip in the fortnight after each start date and recover, you have your answer, and it is the pitch.
  2. Compare your own shifts with everyone else’s. If sign-ups on the shifts you work are markedly higher than the rest, the scheme is not doing the work — you are. That gap is the size of the problem, and it is also the size of the opportunity.
  3. Ask a new starter to explain the scheme back to you. Not “do you know how the card works?”, which gets a yes from everybody. Say: “pretend I’ve never been in — sell me the card.” Whatever they say is what your customers have been hearing. Most owners find this more useful than any number on this page.
  4. Check stamps per visit. If it is drifting above one, you are over-awarding somewhere, and it is nearly always a well-meaning new starter avoiding an argument. The five numbers worth watching in your first 90 days covers where to find this and what else sits alongside it.
Questions

Frequently asked

Does staff turnover affect a café’s loyalty programme?

Almost certainly, because your loyalty scheme is explained to customers by whoever happens to be on the till, and in UK hospitality only about one in four new hires is still there after 90 days, according to the Harri Peach Report 2026. Employer-reported turnover in restaurants and cafés runs at around 39% a year. That means a meaningful share of the people pitching your scheme on any given shift learned it in passing, in a rush, from someone who was also new. No published study measures loyalty sign-ups against staff tenure, so treat this as a reasoned explanation to test in your own café rather than a proven number: look at whether your sign-up rate dips in the weeks after you take someone on.

Why do my loyalty sign-ups drop when I hire new staff?

Because the sign-up depends on a sentence somebody has to say out loud, and a new starter under pressure will drop it first. On a busy morning a day-one hire is concentrating on the till, the order and the machine. The loyalty pitch is the only part of the transaction with no immediate consequence if it is skipped: nobody complains, the queue moves faster, and the omission is invisible to you until you look at the numbers a month later. The people who trained them also explain the scheme in shorthand they have forgotten is shorthand, so what a new starter actually hears is often much thinner than what you think you taught.

How do I train new café staff to run a loyalty scheme quickly?

Give them one scripted sentence, one rule for the awkward question, and nothing else on day one. The sentence should say what the customer gets and what they have to do, in that order, and end with a closed question. The rule should be what to do when a customer asks something they cannot answer, which is to hand over the reward anyway if it is small, and take the customer’s name if it is not. Everything beyond that is better removed than trained: the less of your scheme depends on staff remembering something, the less your sign-up rate moves when your team changes.

How much does it cost a café to replace a member of staff?

There is no reliable published figure for an independent café specifically, and you should be sceptical of the large round numbers quoted by recruitment and software suppliers. What is documented is the direction: training costs across UK hospitality have risen by an average of 70% over the past decade, according to Dojo’s UK Inflation Index, while labour is around 35% of sales, according to the Pineapple and Sona analysis of more than 35,000 hospitality employees. The cost you can actually measure in your own café is the paid hours you spend on each new starter before they can work a shift unsupervised. Multiply that by your hourly rate, then by the number of people you took on last year.

How can I run a loyalty scheme that does not depend on staff training?

Move each step the customer takes off your staff and onto something that never forgets: a sign on the counter the customer can scan themselves, a card that lives in their phone’s wallet rather than in a drawer behind the till, and a reward the till or the app applies automatically. Your staff then only need to be able to say one sentence and point. That is a training job you can finish in a minute rather than a habit you have to rebuild with every new hire.

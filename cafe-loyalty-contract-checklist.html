The Café Loyalty Contract Checklist: 7 Questions to Ask Before You Sign With Any Provider

Every loyalty app demo shows you the nice part: the stamp card on a phone, the dashboard, the free coffee going off. None of them show you the day you want to leave. This guide gives you the seven questions that decide how that day goes, what a good answer sounds like, and a one-page checklist to take into any sales call, including ours.

Last updated 18 September 2026 · UK · Sources: UK GDPR, the ICO, legislation.gov.uk, providers’ published terms

What should I check before signing a café loyalty app contract? Check seven things, in writing, before you sign: who holds your customers’ data and whether there is a data processing agreement; exactly what you can export, in what format, at what cost and for how long after you cancel; the minimum term, renewal date, notice period and any early-exit fee; what happens to your customers’ stamps or points if you leave; every fee beyond the monthly price, including per-message, per-redemption and per-transaction charges; how long onboarding really takes and who does the work; and whether the provider can change the price or terms, and what you can do if it does. A provider that answers all seven plainly is one you can leave, which is the best sign you will not want to.

Before the questions

Why the exit matters more than the features

Features are easy to compare and easy to change later. The contract decides what changing later will cost you. For every question below, get the answer in writing: the terms, the order form or the data processing agreement, not a salesperson’s email.

Your regulars pay for a bad exit
Not just you

When a café leaves a loyalty provider badly, the software bill is the smallest part of it. The real loss is the progress your customers have built up. A regular with eight stamps out of ten does not think “the café changed supplier”. She thinks “they took my coffee away”. People feel a loss more sharply than an equal gain, so wiping out a half-filled card costs you more goodwill than the free coffee ever earned.

That is why the questions below are mostly about leaving. You are not planning to leave. You are checking that you could, because a provider you can leave cheaply has to keep earning your business, and one you cannot leave does not.

You are signing as a business
Less protection than you think

Most of the consumer protection you know from buying a phone contract does not apply here. The Consumer Rights Act 2015 defines a consumer as an individual acting for purposes wholly or mainly outside their trade or business. When you sign up your café, you are the trader. What you agree to is, broadly, what you get.

There is a safety net, but it is narrow. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, section 3 of the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 applies when you deal on the other side’s written standard terms. It stops the provider relying on a term to escape liability for its own breach, or to deliver something substantially different from what you reasonably expected, unless the term is reasonable, and it is for the provider to show that it is. Scotland is covered by a separate part of the same Act. It is a backstop for a dispute, not a reason to skip reading the contract.

Question 1

Who owns your customer data if you leave?

“Ownership” is the wrong word in UK law, but it is the right question. The legal version is: who decides what happens to it?

Controller or processor
The words to listen for

UK GDPR does not give anyone ownership of personal data. It splits the people handling it into two roles. The Information Commissioner’s Office describes a controller as the organisation that decides what personal data is collected and why, and a processor as one that handles it in line with the controller’s instructions.

If the loyalty scheme is yours, you choose the reward, you decide who to message, and the app simply runs it for you, then your café is usually the controller and the provider is your processor. That arrangement comes with real protection for you. Article 28 requires a written contract between the two, and it must say that the processor will, at the controller’s choice, delete or return all the personal data when the service ends. That clause is the closest thing the law gives you to “it’s your data”.

Not every app works that way
Know which model you are buying

Some loyalty apps are closer to a network. Customers create an account with the app itself, collect points that work across many cafés, and the provider decides how that account works. For that data the provider may be a controller in its own right, and it may share only summaries, such as visit counts or anonymised analytics, with each café. The ICO is clear that a processor who uses data outside the controller’s instructions becomes a controller for that part.

Neither model is wrong. A network can be good for customers and cheaper for you. But the two leave you in very different places when you go. Under one, you leave with your customer list. Under the other, you may leave with a report.

What to ask
In the sales call
  • For our customers’ data, are you our processor, a controller in your own right, or both? Which data sits in which category?
  • Where is your data processing agreement, and does it say you will delete or return our data at our choice when we leave?
  • Can we see our customers’ names and contact details, or only totals and trends?
  • Do you use our customers’ data for anything else, such as promoting other cafés?

If you are the controller, you also carry the duties that come with it: telling customers what you collect and why, and handling their requests. Our plain-English guide to loyalty schemes and UK GDPR covers those.

Question 2

What does it actually cost to export your customer list?

“Yes, you can export” is not an answer. The questions are what, how, how often, for how much, and until when.

What a useful export contains
Check the columns

An export you can actually move to another system has, for each customer: a name, an email address or phone number, their current stamp or points balance, the date they joined, their last visit, and a record of whether and when they agreed to hear from you. That last column is easy to forget and matters most. An email address with no record of permission is not a marketing list; it is a list you should be careful about using.

Ask for a sample export file before you sign. A provider confident in its export will send one. A provider that says “we’ll sort it out if you ever need it” is telling you something.

Self-serve, or a support ticket?
And what does it cost

The best answer is a button in your dashboard that produces a spreadsheet (CSV) whenever you like, at no charge. Next best is “email support and we’ll send it within a few days”, with that written into the terms. The worst is an export that is only available as a paid service, or only in a format that works with the provider’s own tools.

A useful habit either way: export your list once a quarter, even when you are happy. It costs nothing, and it means you are never relying on the provider’s goodwill on the day you need it.

The window after you cancel
Often short

Many contracts give you a limited time to take your data after you leave, and then allow the provider to delete it. As one real example, Loyalzoo’s published terms (checked 18 September 2026) say that on termination the merchant may extract its user and transactional data for one month, after which Loyalzoo may dispose of it. That is a reasonable, clearly written clause, and it is exactly the kind of thing to know in advance: export first, cancel second.

Your customers’ right is not your right
A common mix-up

You will sometimes hear that “GDPR gives you the right to your data”. The right to data portability in Article 20 belongs to the individual customer: it lets a person receive the data they provided, in a machine-readable format, and have it sent to another organisation. It does not give your café a right to pull a list out of a supplier. Your right to your customer list comes from your contract, and, if the provider is your processor, from the delete-or-return clause Article 28 requires. So check the contract.

Question 3

Is there a minimum contract term, and what’s the early-exit fee?

The headline monthly price tells you nothing about how long you are committing to pay it.

Monthly, annual, or “annual billed monthly”
Three very different things

A monthly rolling plan can be cancelled at the end of any month. An annual plan is paid up front for a year. The one to watch is the annual commitment billed monthly: it looks like a monthly plan on your bank statement, but you have agreed to twelve payments, and leaving early may mean paying the rest.

Ask directly: “If I sign today and want to leave in month four, what exactly do I owe?” Get the answer as a number.

Automatic renewal and the notice window
Put it in your diary

Fixed-term contracts often renew automatically for another full term unless you give notice before a set date. Miss the window and you may be committed for another year. Because you are signing as a business, you cannot rely on consumer rules to rescue you. Find the renewal clause, work out the last day you can give notice, and put it in your calendar the day you sign.

“Cancel anytime” still has small print
Refunds for part-months

Even genuinely flexible plans usually bill in advance and do not refund the part of the month you have already paid for. That is normal across software, ours included; Loyalzoo’s terms and BaristaCard’s terms both say fees paid in advance are not refunded. It is fair, as long as you know it. What you are checking for is anything bigger: a cancellation fee, a charge to close the account, or a requirement to cancel by post or by phone rather than from your account.

Hardware changes the maths
Tablets, readers, stands

If a provider supplies a tablet or card reader “free”, ask whether it is yours or on loan, and what happens to it if you leave early. Free equipment is often paid for by the length of the contract, and returning or buying it out is where an early-exit cost tends to hide.

Question 4

What happens to customers’ existing stamps/points if you switch providers?

This is the question your customers would ask if they were in the room. Ask it for them.

Two questions, two directions
Leaving and arriving

Ask the provider you are joining: can you import balances from my current scheme, or from paper cards, and who does the work? Ask the provider you are signing with today: if I leave, will the export include each customer’s balance, and can my customers still see their progress after I cancel?

If the answer to the second is “balances aren’t included”, you are agreeing now that a future move will cost your regulars their progress. That may still be a price worth paying. It should be a choice, not a surprise.

A switch that keeps goodwill
A simple plan

1. Export everything from the old system, including balances, before you give notice. 2. Run both schemes side by side for three or four weeks. 3. When a regular joins the new scheme, carry their balance across, or give them a head start that is at least as good. 4. Tell people at the till, on a counter sign and in one message to your members. 5. Set a clear date after which old cards are closed, and honour any stamps a customer can show you until then.

If the old provider cannot give you balances, the fallback still works: honour old cards by eye at the till for a fixed period. It costs you a few coffees. It keeps your regulars.

Coming from paper
The easiest switch of all

If your current scheme is a paper stamp card, there is no contract to leave and no export to request. The only thing to decide is how you honour the cards in people’s wallets. The simplest answer is to add the stamps they already have when they sign up to the new card. We looked at the wider costs of each kind of switch in the hidden cost of a café loyalty app.

Question 5

Are there hidden per-transaction or per-redemption fees?

The monthly price is the fee you see. The fees charged per use are the ones that grow with your success.

Where per-use fees live
Ask about each one

Messages: a charge per text message, or a monthly cap on push notifications or emails, with extra charged above it. Redemptions: a fee each time a reward is claimed. Prepaid sales: a percentage taken on coffee bundles, subscriptions or gift balances sold through the app. Payments: card processing on anything customers pay for in the app. Growth: charges for a second venue, extra staff logins or more customers above a limit. One-offs: set-up, training, printed QR stands, and exporting your data.

Why it matters for a café
Your margin is in pennies

A café lives on small margins per cup. A fee of a few pence per redemption or per message looks trivial in a demo and adds up across a busy month, and it rises exactly when the scheme is working. That is not a reason to refuse per-use pricing; some of it is fair, and a percentage on prepaid sales can be good value if it replaces a monthly fee. It is a reason to see the number before you sign. We worked through what a free coffee really costs you in loyalty reward maths.

What to ask
One request covers it
  • Can you send me your full fee schedule, with every charge that is not the monthly price?
  • At my size, say 300 members and 400 stamps a week, what would my bill have been last month, all in?
  • Are your prices shown with or without VAT?
  • Which of these fees can you change during my contract, and how much notice do I get?
Question 6

How long does onboarding actually take, in practice?

“Set up in minutes” is usually true for the account. It is rarely the whole job.

Separate the account from the launch
Four different jobs

Creating an account and designing a card can genuinely take a few minutes. Getting to a working scheme also means training staff so every barista can add a stamp in the queue without thinking; putting it in front of customers with QR codes, counter signs and a line to say at the till; connecting it to your till, if it integrates; and moving existing members across, if you have any. Ask the provider which of those they do, which you do, and how long each takes for a café of your size.

The morning-rush test
Try it before you commit

The only onboarding that matters is whether stamping works at 8.15 on a Monday with six people waiting. If there is a free plan or trial, run it for a week on real customers before you sign anything long. Count how many seconds a stamp takes and how many customers actually join. We wrote about why sign-ups stall at the busiest time of day in why loyalty sign-ups stall in the morning rush.

Ask to speak to a café like yours
Better than any case study

A provider with happy customers can put you in touch with one: an independent, single-site café that has been using the product for six months or more. Ask them three things. How long did it take before the scheme ran itself? What surprised you? Have you ever tried to get your data out?

Question 7

Can they change the price or the terms after you sign?

A contract you can leave today is only half the protection. The other half is knowing what can change underneath you.

The variation clause
Look for “we may change”

Most software terms let the provider change prices, features and the terms themselves, often with the line that carrying on using the service means you accept the change. That is normal. What makes it fair is notice (how long before a change takes effect), how you are told (email, not just an updated web page), and a way out (the right to leave without penalty if you do not accept a change). On a monthly plan, the way out is built in. On an annual plan, ask for it to be written in.

Some providers also protect existing customers from price rises or free-tier changes. If a provider says so, find the clause.

If something goes wrong
Liability caps

Software terms usually cap what the provider will pay if its service fails you. Loyalzoo’s terms and ours, for example, both cap it by reference to the fees paid in the previous twelve months. For a free or low-cost plan, that cap may be close to nothing. That is not unusual, and for a loyalty scheme it is rarely the deciding factor. It is still worth knowing, because it tells you that your real protection is being able to leave quickly with your data, which brings you back to questions one to three.

Which law, which courts
A quick check

Look for the governing law clause near the end. For a UK café, the law of a part of the UK is what you want. If it names a foreign jurisdiction, or has been left blank, ask the provider to confirm which law applies before you sign.

A one-page checklist to take into any sales call

Ask every provider the same questions, in the same order, and write the answers down. Put the answers side by side before you decide.

1. Customer data

  • Are you our processor, a controller, or both? For which data?
  • Is there a written data processing agreement? Does it say you delete or return our data at our choice when we leave?
  • Can we see customers’ names and contact details, or only totals?
  • Do you use our customers’ data for anything else?

2. Export

  • Does the export include names, contact details, stamp balances, join date, last visit and marketing permission?
  • Is it a self-serve spreadsheet (CSV), at any time, free? Can I see a sample file?
  • How long can I still export after I cancel?

3. Term and exit

  • Monthly rolling, annual, or annual billed monthly?
  • Does it renew automatically? What is the notice period and the last date to give notice?
  • If I leave in month four, what do I owe, in pounds? Any cancellation or equipment fees?
  • Can I cancel from my account, without phoning or writing?

4. Customers’ stamps

  • Can you import balances from my current scheme or from paper cards? Who does the work?
  • If I leave, can my customers still see their progress, and are balances in the export?

5. Every fee

  • Full fee schedule in writing: messages, redemptions, prepaid sales, payments, extra venues or staff, hardware, set-up, export.
  • What would last month have cost a café my size, all in?

6. Onboarding

  • What do you do, what do I do, and how long does each part take?
  • Is there a free plan or trial I can run on real customers first?
  • Can I speak to an independent café that has used you for six months?

7. Changes and law

  • How much notice before a price or terms change? Can I leave without penalty if I do not accept it?
  • What is the liability cap? Which country’s law governs the contract?

Rule of thumb: if an answer is not written in the terms, the order form or the data processing agreement, ask for it to be.

Questions

Frequently asked

What should I check before signing a café loyalty app contract?

Check seven things, in writing, before you sign: who holds your customers’ data and whether there is a data processing agreement; exactly what you can export, in what format, at what cost and for how long after you cancel; the minimum term, renewal date, notice period and any early-exit fee; what happens to your customers’ stamps or points if you leave; every fee beyond the monthly price, including per-message, per-redemption and per-transaction charges; how long onboarding really takes and who does the work; and whether the provider can change the price or terms, and what you can do if it does. A provider that answers all seven plainly is one you can leave, which is the best sign you will not want to.

Who owns customer data in a café loyalty app?

UK data protection law does not talk about ownership. It asks who decides why and how the data is used. If the loyalty scheme is yours and the app provider only runs it on your instructions, your café is usually the controller and the provider is your processor. The law then requires a written contract saying that, when the service ends, the provider will delete or return the data at your choice. Some platforms also run their own customer accounts or networks, and for that data the provider may be a controller in its own right and share only summaries with you. Ask each provider which model it uses, and ask to see the data processing terms.

Can I switch café loyalty providers without losing my customers’ points?

Usually yes, but only if you plan it. You need an export from your current provider that includes each customer’s stamp or points balance, or a record you can check against, and a way to load those balances into the new scheme or honour them by hand. Keep the old scheme running for a few weeks while you move people across, tell your regulars in person and by message, and honour any stamps a customer can show you. If your current provider cannot export balances, the fallback is simple: honour old cards at the till for a fixed period, then close them.

Can I cancel a loyalty app contract early?

It depends entirely on what you signed. Many loyalty apps bill monthly and let you cancel at any time, but usually with no refund for the part of the month already paid. Others sell annual contracts that renew automatically unless you give notice by a set date. When you sign for your café you are acting as a business, not a consumer, so the Consumer Rights Act 2015 does not protect you. The Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 gives some protection against standard terms that are unreasonable, but it is narrow. Read the term, renewal and notice clauses before you sign, and put the notice date in your diary.

What hidden fees should I look for in a café loyalty app?

Look for anything charged per use rather than per month: fees per text message or push notification, per reward redeemed, a percentage on prepaid bundles or subscriptions sold through the app, card payment processing, charges for extra venues or staff logins, hardware such as tablets or printed QR stands, set-up or training fees, and a charge for exporting your data. Ask for the full fee schedule in writing, and ask which of those prices the provider can change during your contract.

Loyalty reward maths

Most cafés copy a stamp card off the shop down the road and never work out what the free one costs. It is not the price of the beans — and once you see the real number, the argument about how many stamps mostly goes away.

Last updated 10 September 2026 · VAT treatment per HMRC Notice 709/1 · Worked in 2026 UK prices

£

How do I set a loyalty reward that doesn't lose money? Start by pricing the reward correctly. A free drink does not cost you the coffee and the cup — it costs you the sale you did not ring up, which on a £3.40 flat white is £2.83 after VAT. From there the test is simple: a completed card pays for itself if it brings in roughly one extra visit that would not have happened otherwise. That break-even holds whether you stamp five or eleven. What the stamp count really controls is how often you pay out — and the biggest saving on the table is not cutting stamps at all, it is capping what the free drink is allowed to be.

The starting point

Why “free after 9” is not a strategy

Almost every stamp card in Britain has somewhere between five and eleven boxes on it, and almost none of those numbers were chosen on purpose.

It was inherited, not decided
The usual origin

Ten boxes fit neatly on a business card, so ten boxes is what the printer suggested, and that is genuinely how most schemes were set. It is not a bad number. It is just not a decision, and it has never been checked against what a free drink costs this café, at this year's prices.

The cost is guessed downwards
“It's only beans”

Ask an owner what the free one costs and you will usually hear a figure around thirty pence — the coffee, the milk, the cup. That instinct is understandable and it is wrong by a factor of nearly ten. It is the single most common mistake in loyalty scheme design.

Nobody checks whether it worked
The missing test

A loyalty scheme is an investment with a return, and the return is extra visits. Very few cafés know whether theirs produces any. Handing a discount to people who were coming in anyway is not loyalty — it is a price cut you did not announce.

The number that matters

What the free cup actually costs you

Take a £3.40 flat white, sold takeaway by an independent in 2026. There are two deductions before you get to your money, and only one of them is the coffee.

1. The taxman is first in the queue
−57p

Hot drinks sold to take away are standard-rated at 20%, so £3.40 on the board is £2.83 in your business. If you are VAT-registered, that is the most you were ever going to keep from the sale. Every calculation from here uses £2.83, not £3.40.

2. The drink itself is cheap
−30p

Roughly 10p of coffee, 6p of milk, and 10–15p for a takeaway cup and lid at trade prices. Call it 30p. That leaves £2.53 of contribution on every paid cup — a gross margin of about 89%, which is why coffee is the business it is.

3. So the free one costs £2.83
Not 30p

Here is the turn. If that customer was coming in anyway, you make the drink either way — the 30p goes out of the door regardless. What changes is that the till does not ring. The reward costs you the whole £2.83 you would have kept. The beans were never the point.

Put differently: one free in ten is a 10% discount, and because your margin is so thin in absolute pence but so wide in percentage terms, that 10% comes almost entirely out of profit. Swap in your own menu price and your own cup cost — the shape does not change.

Worked through

What each stamp count costs per regular, per year

One customer, three visits a week, 156 visits a year, redeeming against that same £3.40 flat white. This is what your scheme is spending on them.

Free after 5 16.7% 26.0 £73.58 29 a year
Free after 7 12.5% 19.5 £55.19 22 a year
Free after 9 10.0% 15.6 £44.15 17 a year
Free after 11 8.3% 13.0 £36.79 15 a year

Each free drink costs £2.83 and each extra paid visit brings in £2.53 of contribution. Note what the last column is telling you: going from free-after-5 to free-after-11 halves what the scheme costs, but it also doubles how long a customer waits for anything — which is the trade you are actually making.

The test

The break-even is always about one extra visit

This is the part that surprises people, and it is the most useful thing on this page.

The sum
£2.83 ÷ £2.53

A completed card costs you £2.83. Every extra visit it causes brings £2.53. Divide one by the other and a card breaks even at 1.12 extra visits. If your scheme makes a regular come in a bit more than one additional time per completed card, it has paid for itself. Below that, you are discounting.

It barely moves
1.08 – 1.22

Run it again with a 20p cost of goods and you get 1.08. Run it with a punishing 50p and you get 1.22. Because the margin on coffee is so wide, your own costs hardly shift the answer — which means you can use this number without auditing your stock first.

And it ignores the stamp count
Same at 5 or 11

Free after 5 and free after 11 both break even at 1.12 extra visits per completed card. The stamp count does not change whether the scheme works — only how often you pay for it. That is why arguing about five versus ten is mostly arguing about the wrong thing.

How to check it without any software: note your average visits per customer per month before you launch, and again three months in. If it has not risen by about one visit per completed card, the scheme is a discount rather than a loyalty programme — which may still be fine, as long as you know that is what you bought.

If the number is too high

Three cheaper fixes than taking stamps away

Adding boxes to the card is the one change customers always notice and resent. People feel a reward being taken away far more sharply than they felt getting it. These cost less and sting less.

Cap what the reward can be
Saves up to 41%

“Any drink free” is the only open-ended promise on your card. Redeemed against a £4.80 speciality drink it costs £4.00 after VAT, not £2.83 — 41% more than you budgeted, chosen by the customer. Set the reward at a fixed value they can put towards anything and the exposure disappears. Nobody experiences that as meanness.

Name a cheaper drink
Saves 23%

A free filter coffee at £2.60 costs you £2.17 against £2.83 for the flat white — 66p a card, or about a quarter of the whole scheme. “A free coffee” reads the same on a card as “a free flat white”, and if you are proud of your filter it is an easier sell than it sounds.

Attach it to something
Adds margin back

Make the reward a free drink with any pastry. Food carries a different margin and a different VAT position, the redemption now brings a sale with it rather than replacing one, and the free drink stops being a pure giveaway. This is the only one of the three that can make a scheme profitable outright.

Keep it in proportion

The reward is rarely your biggest loyalty cost

Free after 9 costs you about £44 a year per regular. That is a real number and worth setting deliberately. But it is worth putting next to the other one: the extra seconds each loyalty transaction adds at the counter run to somewhere between £92 and £275 a month in staff time for a typical single site, and no supplier will ever invoice you for it. If you are auditing your scheme for the first time, the till is where the money is going — the free cup is the part everybody looks at because it is the part you can see.

Questions

Frequently asked

What does a free coffee loyalty reward really cost?

Not the price of the beans. If the customer would have visited anyway, a free drink costs you the whole amount you would have kept from that sale — the menu price less VAT. On a £3.40 flat white that is £2.83, not the 30p or so of coffee, milk and cup. The ingredients get used either way. What changes is that the till does not ring. This is why a reward that looks cheap in stock terms is the single largest running cost of most loyalty schemes.

How many visits should a loyalty reward take to earn?

The stamp count matters far less than café owners expect, because the break-even is the same whatever number you pick. A completed card pays for itself if it produces roughly 1.1 extra visits that would not otherwise have happened — and that figure barely moves whether your cost of goods is 20p or 50p, because coffee margins are so high. What the stamp count changes is how often you pay out. Free after 5 costs a three-times-a-week regular about £74 a year; free after 9 costs about £44. Pick the number you can defend, then cap what the reward can be redeemed against.

Is “any drink free” a risky loyalty reward?

It is the one genuinely open-ended commitment in a stamp scheme. If your reward is any drink on the menu, a customer redeeming against a £4.80 speciality drink costs you £4.00 after VAT instead of the £2.83 you budgeted — 41% more, and entirely outside your control. Naming the reward, or capping it at a set value the customer can put towards anything, removes that exposure without making the offer feel meaner.

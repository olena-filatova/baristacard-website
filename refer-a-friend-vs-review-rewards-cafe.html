Refer-a-Friend vs Review Rewards: Which Grows Your Café's Customer Base Faster

They look like two versions of the same idea: give a regular something, get a new customer. They are not. One of them costs you 28.3p and breaks no rules. The other is prohibited by Google, and in the UK it now runs into consumer law as well.

Last updated 24 September 2026 · Worked in 2026 UK prices · Policy position checked against Google's published rules

?

Should a café reward customers for referrals or for reviews? Referrals — and this is not a close call. Google's own review policy says merchants must not “offer incentives — such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services — in exchange for posting any review”. A free coffee on a stamp card is a free good. So the review-reward option is not a cheaper or riskier version of the referral bonus; it is against the rules of the platform you are trying to rank on. Pay for referrals. Ask for reviews for nothing.

Start here

Most articles compare these two as if they were both allowed

They are not, and that single fact settles most of the decision before you get anywhere near the arithmetic. Here is the rule, in Google's words.

“Offer incentives — such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services — in exchange for posting any review or revision or removal of a negative review.” Listed under prohibited merchant practices — Google Maps user-generated content policy, prohibited & restricted content

Read it carefully, because the wording matters more than the summaries you will find elsewhere. It does not say “cash”. It says free goods and/or services. A free flat white is a free good whether you hand it over as a voucher, a discount code or a stamp on a card — and a stamp is simply a tenth of one. There is no version of “leave us a review and we'll stamp your card” that sits outside that sentence.

The same policy closes the three side doors owners usually try next. Merchants must not pressure customers to review while they are on the premises, must not request that specific content be included, and must not “discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively solicit positive reviews from customers” — that last one rules out the popular tactic of asking how the visit went first and only sending the review link to the people who say “lovely”.

Option A

Refer-a-friend

You reward an existing customer for bringing someone in. Nobody is being paid to say anything in public, so no review platform has an opinion about it.

The reward is conditional on a real transaction — the friend actually buys something — which is also why it is hard to game.

Permitted. Costs 28.3p a side, and only if the card finishes.

Option B

Review rewards

You reward a customer for posting a review. The reward is what Google's policy calls an incentive, and the review is what it calls fake engagement.

In the UK there is a second problem on top: if the reward is not disclosed in the review, it is also a banned commercial practice.

Prohibited. Penalties land on your profile, not on the review.

What it actually costs if it goes wrong

The penalty is applied to your listing, not to the reviews

This is the part that makes review rewards a bad trade even at good odds. Google does not simply delete the reviews it objects to and leave you where you started.

One

No new reviews for a set period

Your profile can be blocked from receiving new reviews or ratings at all — including the genuine ones from the customers who liked you.

Two

Existing reviews unpublished

The reviews you already have can be unpublished for a period. Years of accumulated rating, gone from the listing while the restriction runs.

Three

A warning shown to customers

Your profile can display a notice telling people that fake reviews were removed. Everyone searching for you sees it.

Google says it emails the business before applying restrictions and that there is an appeal route, so this is not instant or silent. But weigh the third one honestly: you would be running a promotion whose failure mode is a public label on your listing saying your reviews were fake. No plausible number of extra reviews pays for that.

And the UK law is a separate question from Google's rules. Under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, in force since 6 April 2025, concealed incentivised reviews are a banned unfair commercial practice, with fines of up to £300,000 or 10% of global turnover, whichever is higher. Note the word concealed: the law does not ban incentivising reviews, it bans hiding the incentive, so a clearly disclosed reward can be lawful. That is a genuinely different line from Google's, which prohibits the incentive whether you disclose it or not. Complying with the law does not put you back inside Google's policy — and Google is where your customers are searching.
The maths

What a referral actually costs you per new customer

With one option off the table, the real question is whether the remaining one is worth running. Work it from the reward, not from the beans.

Flat white, takeaway, menu price £3.40
VAT at 20% — hot drinks to take away are standard-rated −57p
What actually reaches your till £2.83
So one stamp on a 10-stamp card is worth 28.3p
“You both get a stamp” — two stamps, one each 56.7p
Cost if neither card is ever completed £0

That last row is the whole argument. 56.7p is the ceiling, not the price. It is a liability that only becomes a real cost when somebody comes back often enough to finish a card — and a customer who finishes a card is, by definition, the customer you wanted. You are paying for the outcome, at the moment the outcome arrives. Compare that with any advertising you have bought, where the money leaves on the impression and the customer is a hope. The full working behind £2.83 and 28.3p is in what a free coffee really costs you.

Are referred customers actually any good? The honest answer is that nobody has measured this in a café. The best evidence comes from a different industry entirely: a study tracking roughly 10,000 customers of a German bank for almost three years found that customers who arrived through the referral programme had higher margins and, more durably, higher retention that persisted over time — worth at least €40 more each than comparable customers who arrived another way, against a €25 referral fee, giving the bank a return of around 60% on the fee. A bank is not a coffee shop and a three-year customer relationship is not a flat white. Take the direction, not the decimal place: the fear that referral schemes attract freeloaders who bring in worthless customers did not survive contact with the data.

The difference that lasts

One of these leaves something behind. The other is a number on a screen

  Refer-a-friend Review rewards
Allowed by Google Yes — no platform involved No — explicitly prohibited
What you pay for A new customer who bought something A sentence on a website
When you pay Only when a card completes On posting, every time
Cost per unit 28.3p a side, capped Whatever you offered, uncapped
Does it compound? Yes — a referred customer can refer No — a review is posted once
Worst case Some free coffees, no new regulars Reviews unpublished, public warning on your listing

The row that matters over a year is “does it compound”. A review is posted once and then sits there. A referred customer joins your scheme, and can refer someone themselves — which is the only growth mechanic on this page where the output feeds the input. It is also why the referral is worth attaching to the loyalty card rather than running as a one-off voucher: the card is what keeps the new person coming back long enough to become somebody's referrer.

What to actually do on Monday

Run the referral as your paid mechanic, and the reviews for free

You do still want more reviews. You just want them the way the policy allows, which happens to cost nothing and has no ceiling.

Step one

Set the referral at one stamp a side

“You both get a stamp when your friend's first coffee is stamped.” One sentence, works at the till, and the condition is a real purchase rather than a sign-up — which is what stops it being farmed.

Resist making it a free drink each. That is ten times the cost for a reward people barely value more, and it turns a capped liability into an immediate one.

Step two

Ask everyone for a review, once, after the visit

A link on the receipt, or in a message after the visit. Ask every customer, not the happy-looking ones — selective soliciting is prohibited too, and an all-5‑star profile reads as bought anyway.

Don't ask at the counter with a queue behind them, and don't tell them what to mention. Both are named in the policy.

Step three

Keep them apart

The one real cannibalisation risk is bundling. Offering the referral stamp to somebody who just reviewed you converts a clean referral bonus into an incentivised review, and the fact that you called it a referral will not help.

Different moment, different ask, no reward attached to the review. Ever.

How to tell whether it worked. Count referred sign-ups in month one against completed cards in month three — a referral that never turns into a second visit has bought you a transaction, not a customer. Questions owners actually ask

Referrals, reviews and the rules

Should a café reward customers for referrals or for reviews?

Referrals. This is not a close call, and it is not really a marketing judgement. Google's Maps user-generated content policy prohibits merchants from offering incentives — it names payment, discounts, free goods and services — in exchange for posting a review. A free coffee on a stamp card is a free good, so a review-for-stamp offer sits inside that prohibition. A referral bonus breaks no platform rule at all. Reward the referral, and ask for reviews without paying for them.

Does a referral programme work for a small café?

Yes, and it is one of the cheapest things you can run, because you only pay when it works. A simple mechanic — you both get a stamp when your friend's first visit is stamped — costs a ten-stamp scheme 28.3p of future reward per side, and only if those cards are ever completed. Compare that with any form of paid advertising, where you pay for the impression whether anyone walks in or not. The best evidence that referred customers are worth having comes from outside hospitality: a study of roughly 10,000 customers of a German bank found referred customers were worth at least €40 more each and stayed longer, on a €25 referral fee.

Is it safe to reward customers for leaving a Google review?

No. Google's policy is explicit that merchants should not offer incentives in exchange for posting a review, and the penalties are applied to your profile rather than to the individual reviews: Google can stop your profile receiving new reviews for a period, unpublish the reviews you already have, and display a warning on your listing telling customers that fake reviews were removed. That last one is the expensive part, because it is visible to everybody searching for you.

Is offering a discount for a review illegal in the UK?

Incentivising a review is not banned outright by UK law, but concealing the incentive is. Under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, which came into force on 6 April 2025, concealed incentivised reviews are a banned unfair commercial practice, and the Competition and Markets Authority can fine up to £300,000 or 10% of global turnover, whichever is higher. So the law requires clear disclosure, while Google's own rules prohibit the incentive whether or not you disclose it. Meeting the law does not put you back inside Google's policy.

How do I get more Google reviews without breaking the rules?

Ask everybody, ask once, ask away from the counter, and accept whatever comes back. Google's policy also rules out pressuring customers to review while they are on the premises, telling them what to say, and asking only the customers you expect to be positive. A review link on the receipt or in a message sent after the visit satisfies all three, costs nothing, and is unlimited — unlike a reward, which you pay for every time.

Can I run a referral scheme and a review push at the same time?

Yes, and they do not compete, because only one of them involves a reward. Run the referral bonus as your paid growth mechanic and the review request as a free habit at the end of a visit. The one thing to avoid is bundling them — asking for a review and a referral in the same breath, or offering the referral stamp to somebody who has just reviewed you, which converts a clean referral bonus into an incentivised review.

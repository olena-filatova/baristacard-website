Café business rates in 2026: what actually changed

April’s changes were sold as a cut for hospitality, and on paper they are one. But for a lot of independent cafés the 2026/27 bill is higher than last year’s. Here is why, who it hits, what relief you can still get, and how to work out what the rise really means at the counter.

Last updated 14 September 2026 · England · Figures from GOV.UK and the Valuation Office Agency rating list

£

What do the 2026 business rates changes mean for my café? Two things happened on 1 April 2026 in England. The 40% Retail, Hospitality and Leisure relief ended, and a new, lower tax rate for those same premises replaced it: 38.2p in the pound for a café with a rateable value under £51,000, down from 49.9p. The lower rate does not fully make up for the lost discount. If your rateable value stayed the same and you were paying rates last year, your bill before any cap is about 28% higher. The good news: if your café’s rateable value is £12,000 or less and it is your only property, you still pay nothing — and the median café on the 2026 rating list is valued at £11,500.

1 April 2026

What changed, in plain terms

Your bill is your rateable value multiplied by a tax rate (the “multiplier”), minus any relief. All three parts moved this year.

The discount ended
40% relief → 0%

Since the pandemic, cafés, pubs and shops in England had a percentage knocked off their bill. In 2025/26 it was 40%. That scheme ended on 31 March 2026 and is not coming back.

A lower rate replaced it
49.9p → 38.2p

In its place, retail, hospitality and leisure premises now have permanently lower multipliers. A café with a rateable value under £51,000 pays 38.2p for every pound of rateable value; between £51,000 and £499,999 it is 43.0p. Other small businesses pay 43.2p and 48.0p. It is a real, lasting advantage for hospitality — it just replaces something bigger.

Your rateable value was recalculated
2024 rents

The 2026 revaluation reset every property’s rateable value based on what it would have rented for in April 2024. Some went up, some went down. Whatever happened to yours changes your bill by the same percentage, on top of everything above.

So why is it higher?
+27.6%

Last year a café paid 49.9p with 40% taken off — effectively 29.9p in the pound. This year it pays 38.2p. Same rateable value, same café, and the bill is 27.6% bigger before any transitional protection. That is the part the words “rates cut” leave out.

Worked through

Before you pay it

The relief you can still get

Two of these are automatic. One is not, and it is the one worth most to a small café.

Small Business Rate Relief
You have to apply

If your café is your only business property and its rateable value is £12,000 or less, you pay no business rates at all. Between £12,001 and £15,000 the relief tapers down gradually — half off at £13,500. It is not automatic: you claim it from your local council. If your rateable value changed at the revaluation, check your bill still shows it.

Opening a second site? You now keep relief on the first for up to three years, not one, if the second property was taken on from 27 November 2025.

Supporting Small Business relief
Rise capped at £800 a year

If you lost small business relief or the 40% hospitality discount at the revaluation, the rise in your bill is capped at £800 a year (or the transitional cap below, if that is higher) for up to three years, until you reach your full bill. Councils apply it automatically. For the £20,000 café above, that could mean roughly £6,790 this year rather than £7,640 — with the rest arriving over the next two.

Transitional relief
5% cap for smaller sites

If your rateable value went up at the revaluation, transitional relief limits how fast the bill can climb. For properties with a rateable value up to £20,000 (£28,000 in London) the cap is 5% in 2026/27, then 10% and 25% plus inflation in the following two years. Between £20,001 and £100,000 it is 15%, 25% and 40% plus inflation. Also automatic.

A phased rise is still a rise. These schemes decide when you pay the full bill, not whether you do — so plan for the full 2028/29 figure, not this year’s.

Turning it into cups

What the rise means at the counter

A rates bill is an annual number. Your business runs on cups. Converting one into the other tells you how big the problem really is.

The gap, per week
About 13 cups

The £20,000 café faces an extra £1,652 a year once the protection runs out — about £138 a month. A £3.40 takeaway flat white leaves roughly £2.53 after VAT and ingredients. So the rise is about 653 cups a year, or 13 a week. Put another way: about a dozen regulars each coming in one extra time a week. That is a retention job, not a marketing campaign.

Why a price rise isn’t automatic
A second rise in a year

If you already put prices up in April, when the National Living Wage rose, a second rise for rates asks the same customers to absorb both. And it lands hardest on your most frequent customers — the people who feel 20p most because they pay it five times a week. A price rise can still be the right call. Just price it against what you would lose if a few regulars cut back, not only against the bill.

The cheaper lever
Visits you already have

Thirteen extra cups a week is small enough to come from people who already like you. One extra visit a week from a dozen or so regulars covers it, with no price rise of the customers who were coming anyway. That is the case for looking after existing customers first: the maths is small, and you already know who they are.

This quarter

A three-step plan

1. Check your rateable value
15 minutes

Look up your café on the government’s find a business rates valuation service and compare it with similar premises on your street. If the floor area or details are wrong, you can ask the Valuation Office Agency to correct it. It costs nothing to check.

2. Check your bill shows every relief
One phone call

Your 2026/27 bill should list any Small Business Rate Relief, Supporting Small Business relief or transitional relief separately. If your rateable value is near £12,000–£15,000 and there is no small business relief on it, call your council’s business rates team — that one you must claim.

3. Plan for the full bill, in cups
Before April 2027

Work out your full bill without the caps, divide the increase by 52, then by what you keep from an average sale. That is your weekly gap. Then decide how much of it comes from price and how much from getting regulars in a little more often — and give yourself the year the caps are buying you to do it.

Questions

Frequently asked

Did business rates go up for cafés in 2026?

For many, yes, even though the tax rate went down. In England the 40% Retail, Hospitality and Leisure relief ended on 31 March 2026 and was replaced by a lower multiplier of 38.2p for premises with a rateable value under £51,000. Because 38.2p is more than 49.9p with 40% taken off, a café paying rates on an unchanged rateable value faces a bill about 28% higher before any cap. Cafés with a rateable value of £12,000 or less that qualify for full Small Business Rate Relief still pay nothing.

Can independent cafés still get business rates relief?

Yes. Small Business Rate Relief means no rates on a single property with a rateable value of £12,000 or less, tapering to nothing at £15,000, and you have to claim it from your council. Supporting Small Business relief caps the rise at £800 a year for up to three years if you lost relief at the revaluation, and transitional relief limits increases caused by a higher rateable value. Those two are applied automatically.

Does this apply in Scotland and Wales?

No. Business rates are set separately in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, with their own rates and reliefs. The figures on this page are for England. If your café is elsewhere in the UK, check with your local council.

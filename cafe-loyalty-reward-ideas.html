Welcome Stamp, Birthday Treat or Surprise Reward? Which Café Loyalty Rewards Bring Customers Back

Your card works. People collect, people redeem, and it has stopped feeling like it does anything. The usual advice is a list of fifty reward ideas with no evidence and no prices on them. This is three, ranked — by what each one actually changes, what it costs you, and the specific way each one fails.

Last updated 1 October 2026 · UK independents · Costings are illustrative

?

What are the best loyalty rewards for a coffee shop? Three moments are worth funding, and they do different jobs. A welcome stamp gets cards finished — it is the cheapest and the only one with a controlled experiment behind it. A birthday treat grows the basket rather than the footfall: most people who claim one buy something alongside it. A surprise reward keeps momentum in the middle of a card and is the one worth aiming at regulars who are drifting. If you fund one, fund the welcome stamp.

Start here

Every loyalty card has three moments you can spend money on

They are not interchangeable. Each one reaches a different group of people at a different point, and only one of them reaches everybody.

Joining
Reaches: everyone who signs up

The moment a customer takes the card. This is the only reward that reaches 100% of your members, because signing up is the one thing every member does. It is also the moment where the most people are lost: a card that is started and abandoned at two stamps cost you the sign-up and bought you nothing.

The job here is not generosity. It is getting the card finished, because a finished card is a customer who came back nine times.

A date you know
Reaches: those who gave you a birthday

A birthday, or an anniversary of joining. Reaches a self-selected subset — only the people who handed over a date — and reaches each of them once a year. So it will never be your volume lever.

Its job is basket size and goodwill: a small gift on a personal date is remembered, and tends to be bought alongside rather than instead.

The unexpected
Reaches: whoever you aim it at

A free pastry with the seventh coffee. A scratch card that might be nothing and might be a free flat white. A drink on the house for no stated reason.

Its job is momentum — keeping a half-finished card interesting, and giving you something to send a regular who has stopped coming in. It is the most flexible of the three and the easiest to ruin, because the instant it becomes predictable it stops being a surprise and becomes a discount.

Reward one

The welcome stamp: why a free first stamp beats a free first coffee

A free coffee on joining is a discount that ends the moment it is drunk. A free stamp is progress, and progress pulls. There is a field experiment behind that sentence.

The research
Nunes & Drèze, 2006

Two marketing academics ran a loyalty card at a car wash. One group got a card needing eight stamps for a free wash. The other got a card needing ten stamps — with two already stamped on. Both groups had to buy exactly eight washes. The only difference was how the card looked when they were handed it.

The pre-stamped card was completed by roughly 34% of customers, against roughly 19% for the eight-stamp card. The paper’s own summary of why is the useful part, and it is worth quoting exactly: “By converting a task requiring eight steps into a task requiring 10 steps but with two steps already complete, the task is reframed as one that has been undertaken and incomplete rather than not yet begun. This increases the likelihood of task completion and decreases completion time.”

Not only more cards finished — finished faster. The researchers named the effect endowed progress, and it is the single best-evidenced thing in loyalty design.

What to actually do
Lengthen the card, then give one away

This is the bit most cafés get backwards. The lesson is not “make the card shorter”. A nine-stamp card and a ten-stamp card with stamp one already given ask the customer for the same nine purchases — but only the second one is a job already under way.

So: set the card at the length you actually want, and hand over the first stamp as you sign the customer up. The barista scans them in, the stamp appears, and they walk out holding something that has started rather than something that hasn’t.

And it is the cheap one. A free welcome coffee costs you a coffee, today, for every single person who joins — including the ones who never come back. A welcome stamp gives away no product at all. It brings the eventual free drink one visit closer, which is a fraction of a coffee spread across the whole card. The honest caveats
Two of them

First, it was a car wash, not a café, and it was 2006. The mechanism — people finish what they have started — is about as robust a finding as behavioural science has, and it has been replicated across all sorts of progress bars since. But one field experiment at one business is one field experiment.

Second, the full text of the paper sits behind an academic paywall, so the 34% and 19% figures here are the ones consistently reported from it rather than ones we have read off the page ourselves. The quotation above is verbatim from the paper’s published abstract, which is public — and the direction of the effect, and the faster completion, are the authors’ own words.

Reward two

The birthday treat: the reward that sells something else

Judge this one on what goes on the tray next to it, not on how many people come in for it.

What Costa reported
The clearest public numbers

When Costa relaunched its loyalty scheme with a birthday cake reward, it reported that 60% of those claiming their cake bought something else in the same transaction. Around one in ten loyalty members redeemed the cake at all, and 71% of those who did returned within a month. Costa put overall transaction growth after the relaunch at 16%.

Read the shape of that: a low redemption rate, and a high attach rate among the people who redeem. The birthday reward is not a footfall machine. It is a reason to walk in, and then people buy a coffee to go with their free cake.

Do not borrow the magnitude
Chain numbers, chain conditions

Costa has a shop on every high street and loyalty members in the millions. “Returned within a month” is a low bar for a business a customer passes four times a week, and an impossible comparison for a single café on one street. The 16% transaction growth covers an entire scheme relaunch, not the cake.

Take the direction — people who claim a birthday freebie tend to buy alongside it — and leave the percentages where you found them.

The data problem nobody mentions
Day and month. Never the year.

A stamp card needs to know nothing about a person. A birthday reward is the one reward that makes you collect personal data you would otherwise never hold — and under UK GDPR you need a reason for every field you ask for.

Ask for the day and the month only. The year tells you someone’s age, you have no use for it, and holding it means holding more than you need. Say on the form what it is for and that it is optional. Our guide to loyalty schemes and UK GDPR covers what you must get right before you collect a single email.

There is a practical consequence too: the people who give you a date are a self-selecting minority, which is part of why Costa’s redemption rate was one in ten. Plan for a small, warm audience rather than a broad one.

Reward three

Surprise rewards: unpredictable prize, predictable moment

This is the reward with the highest ceiling and the sharpest failure mode, and the difference between the two comes down to one design choice.

Vary the prize, not the schedule
The design that survives

A reward loses its power the moment it becomes expected. But a reward that might not happen at all is a reward most customers never notice. The resolution is to make the moment guaranteed and the prize variable: every stamp earns a scratch, and what is underneath it changes — bonus points, a free drink, sometimes very little.

The customer gets the small anticipation every single time, so the mechanic is visible and learnable. What they cannot predict is the size of the win, which is exactly the part that has to stay uncertain.

How it goes wrong
A surprise on a timetable

Double-stamp Tuesday, every Tuesday, is not a surprise. It is a Tuesday discount, and within a month your regulars have simply moved the visit they were going to make anyway. You have paid twice for the same coffee and changed nothing. Worse, you have taught people that Wednesday is the expensive day.

The same applies to “on the house” gestures that are not actually occasional. A barista who waves a payment through for the same four people every week has not created goodwill — they have created a price those four people now expect. If you want a dated promotion, call it a promotion and cost it like one. Do not file it under surprise.

Where it earns the most
Mid-card, and the drifting regular

Two places. The middle of a card, which is where abandonment lives — the start has novelty and the end has the free drink in sight, but stamp four has neither. And the regular who has quietly stopped coming in, where a small unearned gesture reads as being noticed rather than being marketed at. Our guide to the 21-day window covers the timing of that second one.

One more thing worth knowing if your customers skew young: the barrier for younger customers is overwhelmingly the plastic card, not the idea of loyalty — they join schemes more readily than older customers do. We looked at that in why Gen Z ignores your stamp card.

If you only pick one

Fund the welcome stamp

Not because it is the most exciting. Because of three unglamorous properties the other two do not have.

Why it wins
Reach, cost, evidence

It reaches everyone. Every member signs up, so every member gets it. A birthday reward reaches the subset who gave you a date, once a year. A surprise reaches whoever you aim it at.

It costs the least. It gives away no product — only a small slice of one, spread across a whole card, and only for customers who go on to collect.

It is the only one with a controlled experiment behind it. Same effort, two framings, measurably different completion. The other two rest on one chain’s reported figures and on general behavioural principles — useful, but not the same standard of evidence.

And it fixes the problem most cards actually have, which is not that the reward is too small. It is that cards get started and never finished.

Then add the surprise
Second, not third

If you have room for two, the surprise reward comes next — because it works on the same problem from the other end. The welcome stamp gets the card started properly; the surprise keeps it alive through the middle, where people give up.

The birthday treat is the one to add when you already have a registered customer base worth writing to, and when you want to grow what people spend rather than how often they come. It is a good reward. It is just not the one that fixes a flat card.

In practice

What you can switch on today, and what is still a manual job

Worth being straight about this, because the three rewards are not equally easy to run — in BaristaCard or anywhere else.

Built in
Scratch cards and streaks

The surprise reward is the one that is genuinely automatic. BaristaCard’s Scratch Cards give a scratch after each stamp, revealing bonus beans, a free drink or a perk — the guaranteed-moment, variable-prize design described above, without you having to run anything. Streaks reward visiting in consecutive weeks with bonus stamps at milestones, which is the mid-card momentum problem addressed from a different angle.

A two-second habit
The welcome stamp

There is no “pre-stamp new cards” switch. What there is instead is Customizable Loyalty Deals — you set the stamp count and the reward yourself. So you set the card one stamp longer than you want it, and the barista adds stamp one while they are already scanning a new customer in.

That is a staff habit rather than a setting, which means it needs saying out loud at a handover and writing on a card by the till. It is also the highest-return two seconds in the whole scheme.

Still manual
The birthday treat

Be aware of this before you plan a birthday campaign: BaristaCard does not collect birthdays and has no automatic birthday trigger. Customers can start collecting stamps without registering at all, so for a good number of them you will not hold a name, let alone a date.

Running one today therefore means keeping the dates yourself, lawfully and minimally, and using Promotional Events for a limited-time birthday-week offer or Broadcasts to message your members — noting that a broadcast goes to everyone at once rather than to one person on their day. If an automatic per-customer birthday reward is what you want, that is a feature request rather than a setting, and it is worth asking for.

Questions

Frequently asked

What is a good reward for a coffee shop loyalty card?

The free drink at the end of the card is the reward; everything else is a nudge towards finishing it. If you are adding one nudge, add a stamp at sign-up. It is the cheapest of the three options, it reaches every single person who joins rather than a subset, and it is the only one with a controlled experiment behind it: a card that starts with progress already on it gets finished more often than an identical-effort card that starts empty. Birthday treats and surprise rewards both work, but they work on people who have already joined and are already collecting.

Should I give a free stamp when customers sign up?

Yes, and it is worth being precise about how. Do not shorten the card — lengthen it and give the first stamp away. A nine-stamp card and a ten-stamp card with one already given ask the customer for exactly the same nine purchases, but the second one is a job already begun rather than one not yet started, and that framing is what the research measures. In practice: configure the deal at ten stamps and have the barista add stamp one as they scan a new customer in.

Do birthday rewards work for cafés?

The best public evidence is Costa’s, and it points at basket size rather than at footfall. Costa reported that 60% of customers who claimed their birthday cake bought something else in the same transaction, that around one in ten loyalty members redeemed it, and that 71% of those who did came back within a month. Treat the direction as useful and the magnitude as not transferable: those are the numbers of a chain with a shop on every high street and millions of members, and your single café will not reproduce them. Also note that a birthday reward is the one reward that requires you to collect personal data you would not otherwise hold.

What is a surprise and delight reward?

A reward the customer was not expecting and cannot predict — a free pastry with the ninth coffee, a scratch card that might reveal a free drink, a drink on the house for no stated reason. The thing that makes it work is unpredictability, so the design that survives is a guaranteed moment with a variable prize: the customer always gets the scratch, and what is behind it changes. The design that fails is a surprise on a schedule. Double-stamp Tuesday, every Tuesday, is not a surprise — it is a Tuesday discount, and customers simply move the visit they were going to make anyway.

How much should a café spend on loyalty rewards?

Think in terms of the net cost of the goods you give away rather than the menu price, and set the card length so that the extra visits you need to break even are plausible rather than heroic. A free coffee costs you what the coffee costs you to make, not what you sell it for. The welcome stamp is the cheapest reward of the three, because it does not give away a product at all — it brings the eventual free drink slightly closer.
